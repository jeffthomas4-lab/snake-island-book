## P-120 — ARRIVAL AT THE DRIFTWOOD STAGE

*~350 words. Slight variant per wrapper.*

Chayten's raft slides onto a grassy hill above a half-moon of water. The raft used to be a paddle board. Now it's a small driftwood boat with her initials burned into the bow. Her sandals are still wet from the dock at home.

Below her is the strangest amphitheater she has ever seen.

A natural bowl of grass curves down to the edge of the water. A long flat stage of pale driftwood juts out over the water like a tongue. The seats are stones — not stones placed there, *stones that grew there* — arranged in tidy rows by something that knew exactly how an audience should sit. Some of the stones are big. Some are huge. A few of them, the ones in the back, are larger than her house.

They are humming.

It's a low sound, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum before your ears catch it. The stones are warming up for something. They are an audience that arrived early.

[VARIANT: STAY-TOGETHER. *The other seven kids land beside her. Aiden grabs her hand the way he always does when there's a new sound. Madison is counting heads. Reid is studying the construction of the stage. Carson, who has been to a play exactly twice in his life, says quietly: "This is going to be amazing."*]

[VARIANT: SPLIT. *Three other kids are with her. Aiden is one of them — he asked to come with Chayten, the way he always does, and Chayten said yes, the way she always does. Reid is the other one, positioning himself so he can see both the stage and his cousins at once.*]

[VARIANT: SOLO. *She's alone. The others are elsewhere. Aiden is not here. Her stomach pulls toward wherever he is. She is very aware of how loud the Stones' humming is when there's no one beside her to share the sound with. She has not been this alone in a long time. The humming gets bigger when nobody else is there to soak it up.*]

On the stage, a dozen otters are running in a panic.

One of them — old, gray around the muzzle, wearing a velvet cape and a single small bandage wrapped around his whiskers — is sitting in the middle of the stage looking absolutely calm in the way of a man whose world is ending.

He sees Chayten.

He stands up very slowly.

He says, in a voice like an entire theater season ending: "*You.* You with the EYES. Get DOWN here."

## P-121 — THE BE-A-KID MOMENT

*~300 words. Always read.*

Chayten walks down the grass slope. The wet ones in her sandals squelch.

The old otter meets her at the foot of the stage. He bows. Then he winces, because bowing tugs his bandaged whiskers.

"OTTO," he announces. "Lead actor. Or *was*. My whiskers betrayed me at the dress rehearsal. The Stones go on in forty minutes." He gestures up at the enormous humming audience. "They have been waiting for this play for *eighty years*. They will be FURIOUS if it does not happen. You have the EYES. You will play the lead. Yes?"

Chayten opens her mouth.

She is about to say yes, because that's what she does. Saying yes when other people need something is approximately ninety percent of her personality.

But Otto holds up one paw.

"Stop. Wait. Look at me."

Chayten looks at him.

Otto squints. He tilts his head. He sees, very clearly, the way Chayten has been holding herself — like a chaperone at someone else's party, like a sister who has misplaced her brother for the first time in seven years.

"He is FINE," Otto says.

Chayten says, "Who?"

"The small one. The one you're missing. *He is fine.* He is somewhere doing something brave. You can feel him. Yes?"

Chayten checks, behind her eyes, the place where she can always feel Aiden.

He is, in fact, fine.

He is also, somehow, riding something that is not a horse.

She lets out a breath she didn't know she was holding.

"Good," Otto says. "Now. You are not a sister right now. You are not an oldest. You are not a babysitter. For one hour, you are an *actor*. Do you accept the demotion?"

Chayten laughs. It surprises her.

"Yes," she says.

Otto looks at her for one more long second. Then he says, more quietly: "And child. Before I go. May I just sit here a moment? With you. Not to ask anything."

Chayten blinks. Nobody has ever asked her that. Nobody has ever just *asked to be near her* without needing her to do something for them.

She nods.

Otto sits down on the stage beside her. Cross-legged otter style. He doesn't speak. He doesn't perform. He doesn't introduce his troupe or explain the play or check his bandage. He just sits with her, the way a person sits with another person whose company is enough.

It lasts maybe ten seconds. It is the most important ten seconds Chayten has experienced in a year. Nobody at home knows how to do this with her. They love her so much they always reach for her to *do* something.

Otto stands up. "*Excellent.* Now. Follow me."

## P-122 — CHAYTEN'S CHOICE

*~150 words. The real branch.*

Otto leads her up the driftwood ramp to the stage. The other otters part for her like she's already someone important. One of them tries to hand her a small velvet cape. Another tries to hand her a script.

Otto waves them all off. He turns to Chayten and says, with great formality:

"The play is *The Tale of the Lost Brother.* The lead is the SISTER. You have forty minutes. How would you like to begin?"

## P-123 — PATH A: TAKE THE SCRIPT (BASE)

*~600 words. Yields Voice (base). No ally beyond the troupe in general.*

Chayten takes the script. The paper is soft from a thousand readings. The handwriting is small and looped.

She reads fast. The play is short — six scenes, maybe twenty minutes of stage time. It is about a young otter girl whose little brother was lost in a storm. She spends the rest of her life singing him home, every night, by the water, until she is old. Then she finds him. That's the ending.

It is a sad play.

It is also, Chayten realizes, a beautiful play.

She gets her lines down in twenty minutes. Otto fits her with the velvet cape. Another otter — a fussy one with a measuring tape — adjusts the hem in three seconds flat and tells her she has "*workable*" shoulders.

The Stones are humming louder now. The amphitheater is full. From the back, the audience looks like a quarry someone tipped on its side.

Chayten goes on stage.

She delivers the lines. She is good. She has always been good at being the center of something. Her voice carries. The Stones hum approval at the climactic line — the sister's promise to her brother, every night, that she will keep singing — and when Chayten finishes the final scene, the stones rumble like distant thunder. Otters in the wings weep. Otto, from off-stage, blots his eye with a corner of his cape.

The lights (which are not lights — they are floating bioluminescent moss balls) come up.

Otto walks out on stage and presents Chayten with a small mask carved from driftwood. The mask is shaped like an otter's face but with eyes deliberately too kind. Otto explains that this is the Otter Mask. Whoever wears it speaks with a voice the listener cannot ignore.

"You did well," Otto says. "You delivered our play."

A small otter Chayten hasn't met before — tiny, holding a paper bag — comes up and hands her something. It is a small silver fish.

"For your brother," the small otter says. "He likes fishing. The fish is for him."

Chayten kneels down. "How did you know?"

The small otter shrugs. "You said his name when you got nervous."

Chayten tucks the fish in her pocket.

[CROSSOVER HOOK: *If the reader has played Madison's branch, the small otter pauses and tilts her head toward something far off. "Did you hear that?" she whispers. "Someone is dancing on water." Chayten listens. She thinks she hears, very faintly, footsteps on stone — the precise sound a ballerina makes when she lands.*]

She leaves with the mask in her hands, the fish in her pocket, and the feeling that she did something well.

It was a good show.

It was not a great show.

She is not sure why she feels that.

She thinks she might know.

She doesn't quite say it out loud.

**→ Exit to P-126.**

## P-124 — PATH B: ASK WHAT THE PLAY IS ABOUT (ENHANCED)

*~700 words. Yields Voice (enhanced) + Otto as showdown ally.*

Chayten doesn't take the script yet.

She looks Otto in the eye.

"What's it really about?"

Otto goes very still.

The other otters, who had been swirling around them with capes and combs and measuring tapes, stop. They all turn slightly to look at Otto. As if they are waiting to see what he says.

Otto sits down on the stage. He pats the wood next to him. Chayten sits.

He takes a long breath.

"It is about my brother," he says. "Not the brother I had. The brother I *chose*. We were not born to the same mother. We were not the same kind of otter. He was river. I was lake. We were raised together because his mother died and mine had room. We were brothers anyway. We were not blood. We were better than blood. Do you understand?"

Chayten thinks of Aiden, who is her brother by blood.

Then she thinks of the seven other kids at Sun Lakes. Who are her brothers and sisters in a different way. By choice. By return. By every July for as long as she can remember.

"Yes," she says.

Otto nods. "He was lost in a storm when we were small. The play says the sister sings him home. The truth is, I never sang him home. I just kept singing because I didn't know what else to do."

He looks at her with old wet eyes.

"I have played this play seven hundred and twelve times," Otto says. "Every time, I am the brother who comes back at the end. Every time, I think — *what if this is the time he hears me.*"

He laughs at himself. It is a small, soft laugh, the kind that doesn't ask to be joined.

"The Stones know," he says. "That's why they hum so loud. The play is real."

Chayten thinks for a moment.

Then she says, "Can I change the ending?"

Otto blinks. "Change it?"

"Just one line. At the very end. When the sister finds the brother. Instead of saying *you came home*, can I say *you were always with me*?"

Otto is quiet for a long time.

He says, "I have been playing the wrong ending for seven hundred years."

Then he hugs her. It is the kind of hug only an old otter can give &mdash; small, surprisingly strong, and slightly damp around the edges. His whiskers tickle her ear. He smells like cedar and old velvet.

They rehearse together. He plays the brother. She plays the sister. The new ending makes both of them cry the second time through. By the fourth time they are laughing because they keep crying.

The performance is beautiful. The Stones hum until the ground shakes. The audience of otters in the front weeps openly. Two of them have to be carried out. One says, "WORTH it."

After the play, Otto presents Chayten with the Otter Mask — except this version is older, more worn, more loved. "Mine," he says. "Now yours. Speak with it and the listener cannot pretend not to hear."

He also says, very carefully: "I will come with you to the lake-up-above. To the dock. I have not left The Old Roar in two hundred years. But I'll come for this. For you. For what you did."

A small otter (Pickle, Chayten learns) gives her a silver fish for Aiden. Pickle is shy and won't quite meet her eye.

Chayten leaves with the mask, the fish, an old otter shouldering a velvet cape behind her, and a feeling she will not put into words for several days.

**→ Exit to P-126.**

## P-125 — PATH C: NOTICE PICKLE FIRST (HIDDEN)

*~700 words. Yields Voice (full) + Pickle as small companion + ValuedForPresence flag.*

Chayten was about to take the script.

But there's a small otter shaking by the wardrobe trunk.

She is tiny. Half the size of the other otters. She is holding a costume in both paws — a child-sized brown smock — and she is staring at it like it might bite her.

Chayten kneels down.

"Hey."

The small otter looks up. Her eyes are enormous.

"Hey," Chayten says again. "I'm Chayten. What's your name?"

"...Pickle."

"Pickle. Are you in the play?"

Pickle shakes her head fast. Then she nods. Then she shakes her head again. "I'm — they said I could try out. I tried out. They said I could be the *brother*. The little brother. Just the early scenes. But I can't, I can't do it, I can't go out there, my legs are — they're not — they don't work, they're wrong, I can't —"

Chayten puts a hand on Pickle's shoulder.

"Hey. Pickle. Look at me."

Pickle looks.

Chayten says, "You don't have to be brave to be useful. You just have to show up."

Pickle blinks twice.

"Will you go out there with me?" Chayten asks. "I'm playing the sister. You'd be playing my brother. We'd be on stage together the whole time. I won't let go of your paw. Not for one second. Deal?"

Pickle says, in a voice so small Chayten has to lean in: "...Deal."

Chayten stands up and walks back to Otto.

She says, "I'll do the play. But I want Pickle as the brother. Both scenes. The young one AND the old one."

Otto frowns. "Pickle is too small for the older scenes. The brother grows up. We have a — we have a *taller* otter for that role —"

"Pickle is the brother," Chayten says. "Both halves. Or I won't do it."

The other otters go very quiet.

Otto looks at Chayten. He looks at Pickle, who has straightened her tiny spine half an inch.

Otto says, "I have been mis-casting that role for forty-three years."

He turns and roars at the troupe: "PICKLE IS THE BROTHER. RESET THE BLOCKING. *MOVE.*"

The troupe moves.

Chayten and Pickle take the stage holding paws. Chayten does not let go for the entire play. Not once. Pickle delivers her lines in a tiny voice. The big Stones in the back lean forward to hear — Chayten can FEEL them lean — and when Pickle says her one big line ("I am still here, I am still here, I am still here, I never went away"), the Stones in the back hum so deep that Chayten's chest vibrates with it.

The play ends. Pickle is crying. Chayten is crying. Otto, from the wings, has fallen over.

The Stones hum for a long minute after the curtain.

Otto comes out and presents Chayten with the Otter Mask — the oldest one, the one his great-grandmother wore. "Whoever wears it is heard," he says. "But you didn't need it tonight. You were already heard. That is rare."

Pickle climbs up Chayten's side and clings to her shoulder like a small wet stole. "Can I come with you?" she whispers. "I want to come with you."

Chayten says, "Yes."

Then one of the back-row Stones — the biggest one, the one that hasn't moved in five hundred years — says, in a voice like a quarry waking up: "**SHE SAW THE SMALL ONE FIRST. THAT IS A REAL THING.**"

The whole troupe goes still.

Otto whispers, "The Stones haven't *spoken* in a hundred and twenty years."

Chayten doesn't know what to say.

So she just bows.

The Stones hum.

[CROSSOVER HOOK: *If the reader has played Aiden's branch, the bioluminescent moss-lights above the stage briefly form the shape of a unicorn galloping across the ceiling. Pickle squeaks and points. Chayten looks up. She smiles like she already knew.*]

Pickle gives her a small silver fish for Aiden. (Pickle had been saving it. She had been waiting for someone like Chayten.)

Chayten leaves with the mask, the fish, Pickle on her shoulder, and the weird permanent feeling that the biggest Stone in the back was speaking specifically to her.

**→ Exit to P-126.**

## P-126 — EXIT: THE TROUPE SEES HER OFF

*~250 words. Always read. Slight variant per path.*

The otters line up to wave her off. They are an enthusiastic farewell committee. The fussy one with the measuring tape demands she stand still for "*one more fitting, in case we need you again.*" The wardrobe-trunk one gives her a small velvet square ("*for emergencies*").

The Stones hum, low and warm.

[VARIANT: PATH A. *Chayten waves back and walks toward the meadow. The small otter who gave her the fish stands at the edge of the stage and waves until she's out of sight.*]

[VARIANT: PATH B. *Otto walks beside her, his velvet cape over his shoulder, his bandaged whiskers tilted with new dignity. He hums his brother's name under his breath. Chayten doesn't ask. She just walks with him.*]

[VARIANT: PATH C. *Pickle is on her shoulder. Otto walks beside her, weeping discreetly. The Stones in the back, the big silent ones, hum once more in farewell. Chayten feels it in her teeth.*]

Her boat is waiting in the water below the stage.

She steps in.

She has the Otter Mask (in whatever form she earned it). She has the silver fish for Aiden. She has, somewhere under her ribs, the new and complicated feeling that a play she helped fix was also somehow a play about her own life.

She heads back toward wherever Aiden is.

She always does.

## Editor notes

**On "the be-a-kid moment" (P-121):** This is Chayten's parallel to Madison's float. The structural rule: every region has one passage where the focal kid gets to be a *person*, not a role, before the task begins. Otto sees Chayten holding herself like a chaperone and explicitly demotes her: "for one hour, you are an actor." This addresses the editor note that Chayten risks being "valued for what she does, not who she is."

**On the play's content:** "The Tale of the Lost Brother" is a chosen-family story in Paths B and C. Otto's brother was not blood. They were brothers anyway. This is the same thematic seed Madison's Lake Sisters planted ("the lake is family") — Chayten's region waters it. By the time we get to the showdown, the chosen-family vs blood-family theme has been hit in two regions without ever being lectured about.

**On Pickle (Path C):** Pickle is the deepest payoff. She is the small one Chayten *sees* before she sees her own opportunity. The Stones explicitly say "**SHE SAW THE SMALL ONE FIRST. THAT IS A REAL THING.**" That is the editor's note — "make sure one character explicitly values her presence, not her performance" — delivered by the most ancient audience in the world. The capital letters are deliberate; the Stones are huge.

**On the new ending Chayten writes (Path B):** Otto has played the play 712 times always landing on "*you came home.*" Chayten changes it to "*you were always with me.*" This is a real-life rewrite of grief. It also previews how the showdown will work — the kids don't BEAT the adults' problem. They reframe what's possible.

**On crossovers:** Path A has a Madison crossover (the small otter hears a woman singing on water). Path C has an Aiden crossover (the moss-lights make a unicorn shape on the ceiling). Both are quiet enough to miss; the reader who's played other branches gets the gift.

**On Pickle as an ally:** Pickle goes to the showdown if you took Path C. She perches on Chayten's shoulder. She does not speak. But when Drystone tries to dismiss the kids, Pickle hisses at him — and an adult in the crowd hears a sound they can't quite locate, and decides Drystone is suddenly less credible. Quiet but real.

**On wrapper variants:** Same approach as Madison — short conditional inserts at key beats. Aiden being PRESENT or ABSENT in the wrapper matters more for Chayten than for any other region, because the play she ends up in is about a lost brother. The SOLO variant where Aiden is somewhere else hits harder.

**On word count:** Total ~2,800 words. Path A reader: ~1,500. Path B: ~1,700. Path C: ~1,750. Same shape as Madison's region.

**Open structural question:** I made Otto LEAVE The Old Roar to come to the showdown in Path B. This is a precedent — does any creature get to physically cross over with the kids, or is Pegasus the only one? If Otto can come, can Branch (Madison's ally)? Can Big Coulee? I think the answer is *only allies you EARNED can cross, and they cost something to bring* — but you should make the call before we set it.
