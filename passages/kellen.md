## Passage 150 — Arrival at Skip-stone Lake

![](images/kellen_region.png)

Kellen's raft slides up to the edge of a long flat lake. His raft is now a sleek racing canoe with carbon-black sides. He steps onto the shore and a stone skims past his ear.

Bounces nine times across the water.

He turns.

A flock of bird-creatures stand on the bank, long-legged, blue-feathered, smug. They have piles of stones in front of them and a tiny scoreboard etched into a piece of slate. They are taking turns throwing.

The biggest one, tall as Kellen's knee, with white markings around her eyes, notices him.

"Kid," she says. "This is a CHAMPIONSHIP game. You can watch from over there." She points with one wing at a flat rock thirty feet away.

Just him. He likes it. He always plays better when there's nobody watching him pretend not to think. The lake is enormous. The wind is good. The birds don't know him yet. He can be all of himself before anyone has decided who he is.

Kellen says, "I'd like to play."

All the birds laugh.

The biggest bird says, "This game requires SKILL. And STRATEGY. Most kids only have one or the other. Possibly neither."

Kellen says, "I'll take that bet."

The bird looks intrigued. "It's not a bet. It's a CHALLENGE."

Kellen picks up a stone. He doesn't say anything else. He weighs it in his palm. He turns it over. He checks the flat side against his thumb.

The biggest bird watches him do this. Her name, Kellen will learn, is Skim. She is the kind of bird who notices a person's hands before their face.

"Hmm," she says.

Kellen doesn't look up.

"FINE," Skim says. "The game is called Pattern Skip. I call a pattern. You throw a stone that follows it. Three approved skips, two long, a curve, a stop. That kind of thing. Seven approved patterns. We use them in CHAMPIONSHIP order. You miss one, you sit out."

Kellen nods.

He doesn't say *I'll take that bet* again. He doesn't need to.

## Passage 151 — The Whole-self Moment

Skim hops over (it is more of a hop, with her wings folded behind her like a tiny field general). She holds two stones in her beak.

She sets them in front of Kellen.

"Pick the throwing one."

Kellen looks at them. They look identical to anyone else. They are not identical to him. The one on the left is slightly flatter on the bottom, better skip surface. The one on the right is heavier, better distance but worse curve.

He picks the one on the left.

Skim nods, once. She picks up the heavier stone and skips it across the lake. Nine bounces, then a curve, then a stop. She does this without looking.

Then she does something weird. She picks up the *flatter* stone, the one Kellen would have used, and deliberately throws it badly. The stone skips twice, wobbles, sinks.

She turns to him.

"That's how it goes if I only use my throwing arm. If I forget the rest of me. Did you see?"

Kellen did see.

"I've been doing this for forty years," she says. "You know what I see most? Kids who hide one of their halves. Athletes pretending they're not smart. Smart kids pretending they're not athletic. They think they have to pick. They think people will tease them if they're both."

She tilts her head at him. "You're both. I saw your hand on that stone. I saw you read it. Don't hide it. Eyes, hands, thinking, instinct. The whole kid. Yes?"

Kellen is quiet for a longer time than he expected to be.

Skim is the first creature in a year who has looked at him and seen *both* halves and decided the both was the good part.

He says, quietly: "I haven't wanted to give them the chance."

Skim nods, like she expected this. "I know. Most kids who hide a half haven't. That's why I told you first. So you don't have to hide here."

Kellen swallows.

"Yes," he says. He means it this time.

Skim hops back another step. Her voice goes formal. "You are Kellen the Whole, child. The kid who plays with both halves of himself at once. We do not see many of you. Carry that name."

He picks the throwing stone. He is, for the first time in a year, all the way in his own body.

## Passage 153 — Play the Seven Patterns
Kellen says, "First approved pattern. Show me."

Skim calls it. *Three short, two long, one stop.*

Kellen throws. The stone goes *short short short, long long, stop.* It sits on the surface of the water for a moment before sinking.

The birds blink.

Skim calls the second. *Two long, a curve right, a curve left, two short.* Kellen throws. The stone obeys.

Third. Fourth. Fifth. Sixth.

By the seventh pattern, the birds are no longer laughing. One of them is taking notes on a slate with a tiny pencil. Two others have their heads tilted in the same direction.

Kellen plays clean. He doesn't show off. He doesn't add anything that wasn't asked for. He just *does the patterns,* one after another, exactly the way they're called.

When he finishes the seventh, the bank is silent.

Skim hops over. She's holding a flat stone, the color of slate.

"This stone," she says, "always skips true. It does what you tell it. Skip on water, dirt, paper, glass, anything flat enough to bounce off of. Don't waste it. Don't throw it because you're bored. Throw it when it matters."

Kellen pockets the stone.

Skim says, "And kid, you played with your WHOLE self. You didn't pretend either half wasn't there. That is rare. Most adults have forgotten how to do it."

Kellen says, "I'll try not to forget."

Skim nods once.

*If the reader has played Carson's branch, a baby mammoth thunders past on a distant ridge, briefly visible. Skim follows it with her eyes. "That one runs because she loves it," she says. "Same as you throw because you love it. Different shape, same thing."*

Kellen walks back to his raft. The birds part for him without him asking.


> *This is Kellen's SILVER ending.*

## Passage 154 — Ask Skim to Go First
Kellen says, "Skim. You go first. Let me watch."

Skim's head tilts. The other birds make small surprised sounds. Skim has been the lead thrower for forty years and nobody has *ever* asked her to demonstrate first. They have all wanted to prove themselves immediately.

She likes this kid.

She steps to the line. She rolls her shoulders. She takes a stone, Kellen notices she chose it on weight, not on look, and throws it in the FIRST pattern. *Three short, two long, one stop.* The stone behaves perfectly. It is a small, neat demonstration.

Kellen watches her hand. He watches her hip. He watches where her eyes land before she throws.

She does pattern two. He watches.

Pattern three. He watches.

By pattern four she stops mid-throw and looks at him sharply. "You're learning faster than I'm teaching."

Kellen shrugs. "You're a good teacher."

"I am NOT a teacher. I am a CHAMPION."

"Then you're a champion who can also teach."

Skim is quiet for a second.

Then she does something she has not done in front of the flock in twenty years: she compliments a thrower out loud. "Kid. Throw the patterns. All seven. *Your* way."

Kellen throws. He does all seven patterns. He uses what he learned from watching her, the weight check, the hip turn, the eye-land. He executes cleanly. He adds nothing flashy.

The seventh pattern, he places the stone so it ends balanced on its edge on the shore.

The flock falls silent.

Skim hops over. Her stone, the slate-grey one, is in her beak. She lays it at Kellen's feet.

"I have given this to four throwers in forty years," she says. "Two of them used it well. Two of them lost it. You will use it well. I can already tell."

Kellen pockets the stone. He says, "Thank you, Skim."

Skim says, "And kid. The next time you go to the lake-up-above and someone asks you to play a game with rules, there's a man with clipboards, yes? I have been HEARING about him. The next time he comes around, I'll come too. If you want me."

Kellen says, "I want you."

Skim's flock erupts in tiny dignified bird-cheering. They are all secretly delighted to be invited somewhere.

*Far off, a long deep hum rolls across the lake. Skim's wings twitch. "Standing Stones. They're applauding something. Hasn't happened in a hundred years." She does not explain.*

Skim flies a small loop above Kellen's head as he walks to his raft. The other birds line the bank like an honor guard. The smallest one trips over its own feet and laughs.

## Passage 155 — Invent a New Pattern
Kellen says, "What happens if I invent a new pattern?"

The birds laugh, automatic, dismissive. Skim does not laugh.

Skim says, in a slow voice, "Nobody has invented a new pattern in three hundred years."

Kellen says, "Why not?"

"Because the SEVEN are perfect. They are HISTORICAL. They are APPROVED."

"By who?"

The birds open their mouths to answer. Then they close them. Then they look at each other. Then they look at Skim.

Skim is quiet for a long moment.

She says, "I don't actually know. Nobody does. They've just always been the seven."

Kellen nods.

He picks up a stone. He weighs it. He turns it over. He doesn't say anything for a while.

Then he says, "I'm going to call it *the mirror.*"

Skim leans in. "The what?"

"You throw a pattern. I throw a pattern that mirrors it. Whatever you do, I do reversed. You go three skips and a curve right, I do three skips and a curve left. We make a pattern together that neither of us could make alone. You game?"

Skim does not move for ten full seconds.

Then she says, "Throw."

She picks her stone. They walk to the line together.

Skim throws first. *Three short, a curve right, a long, a stop.*

Kellen throws second. *Three short, a curve LEFT, a long, a stop.* The stones land beside each other on the same flat patch of water, mirroring across an invisible line.

Skim trumpets, for a small bird, she has enormous lungs. The flock goes absolutely silent.

They throw it again. Harder pattern. Mirror. Again. Mirror. Again.

By the fourth round, Kellen is laughing out loud and Skim is making a sound that is not quite a bird laugh but is close enough.

The smallest bird in the flock, the one who trips over its own feet, says quietly: "*We have eight patterns now.*"

The other birds turn slowly toward Kellen.

Skim says, with the gravity of a coronation: "Kid. We have *eight* patterns now. The Mirror is the eighth. It is named after you."

Kellen blushes red to the tips of his ears.

Skim hops to the pile of slate. She returns with the True-Skipping Stone in her beak. She lays it in his palm. Then, and this surprises the entire flock, she lays a second stone next to it. A smaller one, white-flecked. "From me. Personally. For when you need a backup."

She says, "When you go back to that man with the clipboards, I'm coming. *We're* coming. Whole flock. We owe you a debt and we settle our debts."

*A small bright shape gallops across the high air above the lake, a unicorn, glimpsed for two seconds, with a small boy on her back. Skim watches it go. "Oh," she says. "There's another one of those today."*

The flock lines the bank. They bow as Kellen walks past. The smallest one trips over its own feet again. Kellen catches it gently and sets it upright. The smallest one is in love with him forever.


> *This is Kellen's CROWN ending.*

## Passage 156 — Exit

The racing canoe is where Kellen left it. The stones in his pocket clack softly against each other.


He has the stone. He has, somewhere in his chest, the new and weird feeling that he doesn't have to hide either half of himself anymore.

The flock has flown ahead. The smallest bird circles back to wave once.

Kellen pushes off. As he paddles, he says it once to himself, out loud, to make sure he doesn't forget:

"*Played whole. It worked.*"

That's the line. That's the line he is going to remember when he is fifteen, and twenty, and forty. The line that finally tells him the truth about himself.

He paddles toward the sound of the C-130, faint and far, somewhere up in the world above.
