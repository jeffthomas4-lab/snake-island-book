Note for the reader
This is a choose-your-own-adventure book. Each of the eight kids' regions in Act 3 shows three paths — for a first read aloud, pick one path per kid. The other paths are there to discover on a second or third readthrough. The showdown in Acts 4 and 5 contains a main version and a fallback for each kid — read the main versions on a first read; the fallbacks are for replays where the reader skipped certain paths. The sidebar lets you jump to any chapter. A complete first reading is about nine bedtime sittings; a full readthrough with all variants is about thirteen.

Chapter 1 — Arrival at Sun Lakes

The first day of the trip

P-001The Synchronized Jump

The truck doors open at the same time.

It is the third of July. The drive from the western side of the state took most of the day. The last twenty minutes was the long winding downhill road — narrow, no shoulder, dropping into the canyon — and you'd miss the turn entirely except for the dented unmarked mailbox at the top of it. Every year somebody in the lead car panics that they've gone past. They haven't. There it is.

The dirt parking lot is at the bottom of the hill. Eight kids tumble out of four cars, into dust and dry grass and the smell of warm rock. Sun Lakes sits below them. The cove is the bluest it's ever been.

The grown-ups are still in the parking lot arguing about who made the best time. (The mom from Madison and Kellen's car insists she did. The dad from Carson and Keira's car disagrees on principle. Uncle Bill, sitting on the tailgate of his truck, is refereeing badly.) They are also arguing about what time to start the pizza for tonight. Pizza is the first-night dinner. It has been the first-night dinner for as long as any of them can remember. The argument isn't about whether — it's about when to put the first one in the oven, which depends on how hungry the kids are, which depends on how cold the lake is, which depends on a thousand things nobody is going to actually figure out before someone just goes ahead and turns on the oven.

The dock is still there. (Reid checks. He always checks. He noticed last summer that the third plank wobbles. This summer the wobble is a little worse. He doesn't tell anyone. He never does. But he files the difference under the same heading he files everything: things that get smaller every year if you don't pay attention.)

The kids run. Not toward the condos. Not toward the bags. Toward the dock.

This is the rule. The rule is older than any of them. The rule has no rule-maker. It just is.

When you arrive at Sun Lakes, you put your bag down and you run to the dock and you all jump in at the same time.

Madison hits the dock first, because she's the oldest and her legs are longest. She kicks her sandals off as she runs and they land in the grass behind her. Carson is next, a half-step behind. Then Reid, then Chayten and Kellen at a near-tie, then Keira (whose legs are catching up to the older kids each year and who is going to be faster than all of them in two more summers), then Ella in her bright pink swimsuit, then Aiden, last, the smallest, his bare feet slapping the wood.

This year is different for Aiden. He learned to swim this spring. Really learned. He spent six weekends at the YMCA pool with his mom and now he can do an entire lap. He still wears his bright orange life jacket every time he hits the water — Mom's rule, and his rule too. Buckled tight at the chest, leg-strap clipped, the way she showed him. Last summer he was the kid the grown-ups had to bring in, careful, double-checked, with the jacket on backwards because nobody had shown him yet. This year he knows how to buckle it himself, and he is running to the end of the dock with the rest of them, and nobody is reaching for him because nobody has to. He is going to jump in with his cousins. By himself. For the first time. And nobody is going to ask if he's sure.

He is sure.

They line up at the end of the dock. Eight kids in a row. The wood is hot under their feet. The water is so clear they can see the brown shadow of the dock pilings underneath. A heron stops fishing in the shallows and looks up at them like here we go again.

Madison says, "Ready."

Reid says, "Ready."

Carson says, "READY."

Chayten says, "Ready."

Kellen says, "Ready."

Keira says, "Ready."

Ella says, "Ready."

Aiden says, "Ready."

They count to three out loud, all together.

ONE.

TWO.

THREE.

Eight kids jump at once.

The splash is enormous. Eight bodies hit the water in the same second. Carson does a cannonball that is louder than physics allows. Madison goes in feet-first, ballet-clean. Reid hits the water like a tree falling. Chayten and Kellen go in side by side. Keira does a half-twist she's been practicing. Ella jumps with her arms straight up and her crown of pink hair-ties intact. Aiden jumps with his eyes closed, his orange life jacket bright against the cliff shadow, his hand reaching for Chayten's and finding it under the water. The jacket pops him back up first.

They surface together. Eight heads bobbing. Eight grins. The lake is exactly as cold as it always is, exactly as warm at the surface, exactly the right kind of here that means the trip has started.

Madison spits water. "We're here."

Aiden, breathing through his nose because his sister's hand is still around his and he refuses to let go, says, "We're HERE."

The grown-ups are walking down the path now, carrying bags, still arguing about coolers and pizza times. They look up and they see eight kids in the water and they smile at each other in the specific way grown-ups smile when they have done one thing right.

Tonight is pizza night. The big house won't smell of brisket until tomorrow, or the night after — Uncle Bill's BBQ does its slow work for the middle of the trip. But the porch and the side yard still hold the ghost of brisket from years past. The wood is soaked in it. The siding is. The cushions on the deck chairs are. Even after a year of rain and snow and canyon wind, the place still smells faintly of every brisket Uncle Bill has ever cooked here. Memory has a smell. So does Sun Lakes.

The bats are not yet out. They will be, tonight, after sunset, swooping low to eat the canyon mosquitoes. The wind is not yet howling. It will, after dark, when the canyon breathes the cold air down from Grand Coulee Dam.

But for now: just water. Just sun. Just eight kids in the cove on the third of July, exactly where they belong.

The summer has started.

The summer always starts this way.

P-002Mr. Drystone Arrives

It happens later in the afternoon.

The kids have done the things kids do at Sun Lakes in the first hours: unpacked into the same condos they always have (each family has the same condo every year; nobody decided this, it just is), grabbed snacks from the cooler, and scattered. The pizza dough is rising on the counter in the big house. The grown-ups are still in there laughing at something none of the kids will ever know.

Madison is reading on the grass with a tin of cookies beside her. She always brings cookies on the first day — or, some years, makes them during the trip. This was a bring-them year. She made them last night. They are oatmeal-chocolate-chip and they have a small amount of cinnamon in them that nobody but Aiden ever names.

Chayten is paddle-boarding back from the back dock with Aiden, who is sitting cross-legged on the front of the board holding a fishing rod and talking to it.

Reid is at the edge of the main dock, whittling a stick.

Kellen is in the grass taking shadow swings with a baseball bat. He is not hitting anything. He is doing the thing where you look out at the far-off island on the other side of the lake and you visualize the perfect arc — pitch in, weight back, hips through, contact, follow-through, the ball sailing all the way across the cove and landing on that island. He has hit that island in his head about forty times today. He is going to keep hitting it.

Carson is on the grass with a putter, hyping a mini-golf rematch for after dinner.

Keira is sketching the lake in a notebook with a small soft pencil.

Ella is sitting on the porch with her chin on her hands. She is just sitting. She is not putting on makeup, not playing a game, not doing anything in particular. She is with her cousins. All week. No school. No piano. No one telling her what's next. She is thinking about exactly nothing, and it is the best feeling she has had all year.

Aiden is, as established, talking to his fishing rod. ("Wait. Wait. Fish are in WATER. How am I going to fish? I can't fish from the dock. I have to be IN the water. But if I'm in the water I'll scare the fish. This is — Chayten. CHAYTEN. How do people FISH.")

The man arrives in a white pickup truck.

He pulls into the dirt parking lot. He gets out. He is wearing a hard hat. In a wide open dirt parking lot. That is the first weird thing. He has three clipboards under one arm and a tablet in his other hand. He walks down to the dock without looking up from the tablet.

Carson sees him first. Carson says, "Uh."

The man does not introduce himself. He pulls out a sign on a metal stake and hammers the stake into the grass at the head of the dock. He uses a small mallet. He hits the stake four times. He does not look up.

The sign reads:

COMING SOON
DRIEDMORE DATA CENTER — WEST CASCADIA HUB
GROUNDBREAKING JULY 4 — 12:00 NOON
THIS COVE IS UNDER CONTRACT TO DRIEDMORE PROPERTIES

Madison sets her book down. She walks over. She reads it twice. She knows what a data center is. She knows what they need.

Carson asks. Madison says, "They use a LOT of water. Cooling. They want to drain the lake."

Aiden, paddle-boarding back, says, "But we can't have a lake if there's no water."

Madison says, "That's the whole point."

The man finally looks up. He sees a small group of kids standing around his sign. He sighs.

"Don't TOUCH the sign, kids. This here is a malfeasance of zoning. I am Mr. Drystone of Driedmore Properties." He pronounces ecosystem "echo-system" without noticing.

Reid says, quietly, "My family has come here for years. Why are the other people selling?"

Drystone laughs. "Selling? Sweetheart, the people who own this place have a dock with NO foundation. The water's eaten the ground out from under it. They can't AFFORD the geological work. Driedmore is RESCUING them. We're the good guys here."

(The rental families who come every summer don't own a square inch of Sun Lakes. They have rented the condos every July for as long as the kids have been alive. The cove belongs to the family who runs it. The rental families just belong to the cove. There's a difference. There's not really a difference.)

He scans something on his tablet. He does not look at any of the kids' faces.

He leaves.

P-003The Kids Can't Fix This

The kids stand around the sign for a long minute.

Reid looks at the dock. The third plank is wobbling under his weight more than it did even last summer. He bends down. He looks at the pilings underneath. He can see, in the clear water, the way the dirt around them is being pulled by the current. The dock foundation IS being eroded. Drystone is right about that part.

The kids try the obvious things.

Madison wants to find the adults. The adults are inside, finishing the lunch dishes and starting prep for tonight's brisket. Uncle Bill is asleep on the couch in the sunroom. (Bill has been up since four AM. He is allowed.) Madison does not wake him. She also realizes, as she's standing outside the door, that her own mother has been talking about "the situation" all week on the phone. The grown-ups already KNOW about Driedmore. They have been talking about it for weeks. They have been quietly considering it because of the dock.

Reid suggests pulling the sign down. Carson says no — that's not how you win. That just makes them put up another sign and now you also have a vandalism charge.

Kellen suggests calling somebody. Keira points out they don't know who.

Aiden, very quietly, says, "Can we just ask the lake to stop it?"

There is a pause. Several of the older kids laugh, but the laugh is not unkind. Madison doesn't laugh at all. She looks at Aiden. Then she looks past him.

She looks at the sunroom.

The door is half-open. Inside, the rafts are piled up against the wall, drying from this morning's lake-jump. The big inflatable unicorn is on top of the pile.

The unicorn is bobbing.

There's no wind.

P-004The Sunroom

The kids drift toward the main house.

The sunroom door is half-open. The big inflated unicorn is still bobbing.

Aiden walks in first. Aiden is seven and has not yet learned to be afraid of weird things.

The other seven follow.

The sunroom is warm — warmer than the rest of the house. The sun has been on it all afternoon and the wall of windows has trapped the heat. The plastic of the inflatables is warm against the skin. The smell is the specific smell of Sun Lakes in July: the deep-soaked brisket-memory of the side yard, sunscreen on the kids' shoulders, lake water still on their suits, warm plastic, the faint sourdough smell of pizza dough rising in the kitchen, and one other smell that nobody can name — cold and wet and faintly green, like cave water on a hot day, even though there is no cave water in the sunroom.

Madison says, "Is that smell new?"

Reid says, "It feels new."

Chayten sets the paddle board against the wall. Kellen leans the baseball bat against the doorframe. Carson pockets his putter. Keira closes her sketchbook. Ella stands up from where she's been sitting.

Eight kids are now standing in the sunroom, surrounded by inflatables, with no clear plan.

Aiden walks up to the unicorn raft. He places one small hand on its plastic side.

The unicorn raft is warm.

The unicorn raft tilts its big inflatable head toward him.

Aiden does not flinch. He just nods, like the unicorn raft has confirmed something he already suspected.

Outside, somewhere up the canyon, a sound begins. It is far off and getting closer. The sound is enormous and low and the kids feel it in their chests before their ears catch it.

The C-130.

It comes through the canyon a couple of times during their week here. Not every day. Just a handful of times during the trip, the giant gray cargo plane swinging low between the cliffs because the canyon is the perfect training run. The kids look forward to it. The grown-ups pretend not to. When somebody yells PLANE! every kid in earshot drops what they're doing and runs to the deck to watch.

But today is different. Today the sunroom is humming.

P-005The C-130

The C-130 thunders past.

It is so loud the windows rattle. The frame of the sunroom shakes. The pile of inflatables shifts. Aiden grabs Chayten's hand. Reid grins. Carson covers his ears and laughs. Keira instinctively shields her sketchbook even though it's already closed. Ella's hand mirror falls off the windowsill and onto the carpet without breaking.

The plane is huge. Through the sunroom windows, the kids can see it for a full second — gray and low and almost stupidly close to the cliffs — and then it is past, the sound trailing behind it as it disappears around the bend.

The sunroom is still humming.

The pile of inflatables is no longer a pile.

The rafts have moved.

The unicorn raft is on the floor in front of Aiden. The canoe-shaped raft is in front of Madison. A long log-shaped raft is in front of Reid. A sturdy wheeled-boat raft is in front of Carson. A racing canoe is in front of Kellen. A flat painter's raft is in front of Keira. A small boat with golden trim is in front of Ella. A driftwood-bowed boat is in front of Chayten.

Each kid is standing in front of a raft.

Each raft was waiting for them.

Nobody says anything for a moment.

Then Aiden climbs onto the unicorn raft.

He doesn't think about it. He just does.

The unicorn raft lifts off the floor.

The walls of the sunroom go away.

The lake outside is no longer the lake outside. There is a bigger water. A louder water. Madison can hear it — a big sound, far off, like a waterfall. Like the BIGGEST waterfall.

Each raft starts to slide off in a different direction. The kids try to call to each other but the wind is loud. Aiden, on his unicorn, is laughing. Madison, in her canoe, is shouting instructions. Reid is calmly steering. Carson is whooping. Kellen is grinning. Keira is open-mouthed. Ella is sitting perfectly straight with her hands folded in her lap as if she has been waiting for a royal carriage and one has finally arrived.

The last thing each kid hears is Madison's voice, far off: "WHATEVER YOU DO, STAY ON YOUR RAFT UNTIL IT LANDS."

(Madison is the oldest. She always knows.)

The rafts pull them through. The sunroom is far behind them. The bigger water is below them. The C-130 is somewhere far away in a world they have just, briefly, left.

Chapter 2 — The Old Roar

Crossing into the memory-world

P-010Eight Rafts Land in the Meadow

The rafts land together.

Not where they were heading separately — somewhere new. A green meadow under a sky that is the color of green water seen from underneath. The grass is the brightest green any of the kids have ever seen. The air smells like rain that has not happened.

They climb off their rafts. They stand in a loose group. They check that everyone is here. Madison counts heads. Eight. Good.

A roaring sound is filling the meadow from every direction. It is the sound Madison heard from her canoe — bigger, louder, more present. It is a waterfall. It is every waterfall.

In front of them, where the meadow falls away, they can see why.

Dry Falls is wet.

Dry Falls — the enormous cliff at the head of the canyon, the one that is in every school field trip the kids have ever taken in this part of the state, the one that has been a dry falls for fifteen thousand years — is roaring. Water coming over the lip in a sheet a thousand feet wide. The sound is the sound of geology happening.

Aiden whispers, "Oh."

Carson whispers, "Whoa."

Madison says, "Where are we?"

A voice from behind them, warm and low and amused, says, "Lake family. Good. Been too quiet around here."

The eight kids turn.

P-011The Harbor Keeper

He is sitting on a log at the edge of the meadow.

He is large. Not big-as-a-tree-being large, not big-as-a-mammoth large. Just human large — broad shouldered, a little stooped, the size of a grown-up uncle who has spent a lifetime carrying things. He has a beard the color of cedar bark. He is wearing what looks like a faded denim shirt and suspenders. He has work boots. He has a beat-up canvas hat pushed back on his head.

He is whittling.

A small pile of wood shavings has gathered at his feet.

He looks up as the kids turn.

He smiles.

The smile is specifically familiar.

Reid notices it first, in the part of his brain that notices things without telling anyone. The smile is one he has seen before. He can't quite place where.

The man says, "Sit down, sit down. Don't stand around looking like you got off a bus."

He gestures to a half-circle of flat stones around the log. They are the perfect height for sitting. They were not there a second ago.

The kids sit.

The man keeps whittling. He does not introduce himself for a while. He just works. The shavings curl off his knife in long thin spirals. Aiden watches with the kind of attention Aiden gives to things that are mysterious and excellent.

After a long minute, the man says, "You can call me the Harbor Keeper. That's the name I have on this side. I have had others. Last visitor I had, a long time ago, called me something else. I don't remember what. I have a bad memory for what people call me. I have a good memory for the people themselves."

Madison says, "What... what is this place?"

The Harbor Keeper looks up. "You're in The Old Roar. That's the name for what's underneath your cove. Underneath every place that gets loved enough, long enough, by the same families coming back year after year. The Old Roar is the memory of the land. It is the attention the land remembers being given. Your families have been giving this lake attention for a long time. The lake remembers. So do I."

He puts the whittling down. He looks at each of the kids in turn.

He says, "Madison. Carson. Chayten. Reid. Kellen. Keira. Ella. Aiden."

Nobody told him their names.

The kids do not react the way kids in movies do. They do not gasp. They just sit there and absorb it. Aiden, characteristically, just nods like he already suspected.

The Harbor Keeper says, "There is a problem at your dock. I know about it. The lake knows about it. The Old Roar is connected to your cove and what happens up there happens down here. If your lake gets drained, this world ends. So. We need to fix it."

He stands up. He stretches. His knees crack like old wood.

"Each of you can earn something here. Something that will help up there. There are eight things to find, one per kid. None of you can earn another kid's thing. Each one is shaped for you specifically. The Old Roar already knows what you need. You'll find it because the world will lead you to it."

He turns and gestures across the meadow. The grass behind him opens out into eight different regions, visible all at once — a quiet pool, a driftwood stage, a wide grassy plain, a rocky woodland, a long flat lake, a basalt cliff, a moss circle, a meadow with a sideways-growing rainbow.

The Harbor Keeper says, "But. There's a thing about time."

The kids look up.

The Harbor Keeper says, "You'll have time for four."

He says it plainly. He lets it sit.

"Four regions, if you stay together. All of you in one group, going to four places, deep. You won't get to all eight. You'll have to choose which four matter most."

He pulls a worn deck of cards out of his shirt pocket and shuffles it once, absently, the way some grown-ups always have a deck of cards on them.

"Or," he says, "you split up. Two groups of four. Cover all eight regions but each kid sees less of each one."

He fans the cards. Holds them up.

"Or each of you goes alone. All eight regions covered, deepest possible. Hardest. Bravest. I won't tell you which to pick. That part's yours."

He puts the cards back in his pocket.

He says, "You want to play gin rummy after, that's fine by me. I've got time."

He winks at no one in particular.

Reid is staring at him. He still can't place the smile.

P-012The Strategy Choice

The Harbor Keeper steps back. He sits down on his log. He picks up his whittling.

He waits.

The eight kids look at each other.

Madison says, "We have to decide."

Carson says, "What do you guys think?"

Aiden, who is seven, says, "Whatever Madison says."

Madison says, "It's not just my call, buddy. We all decide."

The eight kids stand in a loose huddle. The Harbor Keeper is whittling on his log, not watching them, giving them space. Dry Falls is roaring in the distance.

Madison says, "Okay. Three choices. We stay together and go deep on four places. Or we split into two groups and cover all eight. Or each of us goes alone — all eight regions, deepest possible."

The reader chooses how the kids will approach The Old Roar.
  • Stay together — 4 regions deep, all 8 kids along. Safest.
  • Split into two groups — 8 regions covered, groups of 4. Balanced.
  • Each goes alone — 8 regions, deepest possible. Hardest. Bravest.

In this reading copy you'll see each region with all three wrapper variants (Stay-Together / Split / Solo) and all three path choices (A base / B enhanced / C hidden). In a real playthrough you'd pick one of each.

The eight regions follow. Read them in any order — the kids visit them all.

Chapter 3 — Madison at the Quiet Pool

Item earned: The Map · Possible allies: Branch the beaver, the Lake Sisters

P-110Arrival at the Quiet Pool

Madison's raft slides to a stop on the shore of a pool so still you could roll a coin across it.

The raft is a wooden canoe now, with her name carved into the side: MADISON. The tin of cookies she was holding in the sunroom is still in her lap. None of them are crushed. (She checks.)

The pool is shaped like a heart, if a heart had been drawn by someone who'd never seen one. Rocks at the edge. Cottonwoods leaning over the water. A heron standing on one leg in the shallows, looking at her like she's late.

If the group stayed together The other seven kids are climbing out of their rafts behind her. Reid is already studying the rocks. Aiden is whispering something to a frog. Carson is sizing up the heron like he wants to race it. Madison turns and counts heads — eight. All present. Good.
If the group split into two Three other kids are climbing out behind her — the group she came in with. They give her space. They know this one is hers.
If each kid went alone She's alone. The other seven landed somewhere else. The silence is bigger than she expected. She steadies herself. Oldest kid. She knows what she's doing.

Three heads pop up out of the water.

Three girls. One her age. One younger. One small.

Their hair drips upward instead of down.

The oldest one tilts her head. "Are you the cookie-bringer?"

The middle one says, "She IS the cookie-bringer."

The youngest one is already chewing on something.

The oldest climbs out. Her feet leave wet prints that turn into flowers. "I'm Pip. That one's Lark. The one chewing is Wren. Wren, what are you eating."

Wren opens both hands. They're full of pondweed she's mashed into the shape of a cookie. It is the saddest cookie Madison has ever seen.

"It's my OWN recipe," Wren says.

"It's TERRIBLE," Pip says.

Madison opens her tin. The cookies inside smell like brown sugar and brisket smoke and home.

She is about to share them.

Then Lark says: "Wait. First — sit down."

P-111The Cared-For Moment

Lark says it again. "Sit DOWN. You've been holding it together for everyone for three days. We can tell."

Madison opens her mouth to say she's fine.

Pip says, "Don't."

Wren tugs her hand. "Float."

Madison doesn't quite understand. Pip puts a hand on her shoulder and shows her — the pool will hold you up, if you let it. The water here is the kind of water that knows what tired is.

Madison sits. Then lies back. Then floats.

For one minute — sixty whole seconds, no one counting but everyone knowing — Madison just floats.

The sky above the pool is so green it isn't a sky color. There are no clouds. There's a sound somewhere that might be wind or might be humming. The water is warm at her back and cool at her elbows. Wren puts a pondweed cookie on her stomach. Wren is very proud of this gesture.

Nobody asks Madison anything.

Nobody needs anything from her.

Then the pool does something it has not done in a hundred years. It shows her a memory it has been saving.

It is just a flash. Three seconds, no more. Madison sees the lake at home, from underwater — the wobbly shimmer of the surface, and above it, eight pairs of legs all in a row at the end of the dock. Eight pairs. Her cousins' legs. Her own legs. The legs are bending. The legs are jumping. The water erupts above her with eight bodies arriving at once and the sound is the sound of the trip starting, the way it has every July of her whole life.

The memory fades.

Madison's eyes are wet for a reason that has nothing to do with the Sisters. The pool gave her that. For no reason. As a gift. Because she was floating.

She is fifteen years old and someone is taking care of her.

When she sits back up, her hair is dripping upward, just a little, like the Sisters'.

Pip nods. "Okay. NOW you can be in charge again."

Madison laughs. It's a real laugh. The kind she usually saves for inside her own head.

The Sisters wait. They've done their part. The rest is hers.

Pip says, "We have something to tell you. About the lake-up-above. About the man with the clipboards."

Madison sits forward.

She is, in fact, ready now.

P-112Madison's Choice

Pip tells her the rest fast.

The Lake Sisters have been watching the lake-up-above for years. They've seen Mr. Drystone's boats. They've seen him drop papers into the water — papers they were never meant to read. The papers sank. The Sisters caught them. They have them now.

But the papers are in three pieces, and only one piece is here at the pool. The other two are elsewhere.

Pip is holding the first piece. It glints in her hand.

"How do you want to do this?" Pip asks.

Madison thinks for a second.

The reader chooses Madison's path.
  • Path A (base) — Take the piece Pip has and head back. The fast path. You'll have a Map. Not the whole map, but enough.
  • Path B (enhanced) — Ask the Sisters what THEY need from YOU first. There's a story there. It might cost time. It might be worth it.
  • Path C (hidden) — Notice that Wren has gone quiet and ask her what's wrong. Wren is small. Most people don't see her. You see her.
PATH A — SHARE THE COOKIES, TAKE THE PIECE (BASE)

Madison opens her tin and shares the cookies fairly. One for Pip. One for Lark. The biggest one for Wren, because Wren is smallest.

(Wren cries with joy. Lark and Pip do not comment. They've seen Wren cry over a particularly good rock.)

The Sisters eat their cookies the way creatures eat who have never had anything baked before. Pip nibbles. Lark inhales hers in two bites. Wren tries to make her cookie last by licking it.

When they're done, Pip looks at her sisters. The three of them nod at each other in the way of people who have just received something and now have to give something back. (That's how exchanges work in the Old Roar. You bring something. They bring something. That is the whole agreement.)

Lark slips back into the pool and comes up holding a small, perfectly round, lavender-colored stone. It is warm in her hand. It is glowing very faintly, the way a smooth river rock looks when a kid pulls it out of shallow water and holds it up to the sun.

"For you," she says. "It's a quiet stone. Hold it when a place is too loud. It pulls the noise into itself and gives you back about ten seconds of quiet. Sisters made it. It only works if you got it from a sister."

Madison closes her hand around it. It is exactly the kind of thing she did not know she needed and has needed her whole life.

Wren hands her something next. It is a small pondweed-and-pebble bracelet she has clearly been making during the cookie sharing. It is, like Wren's cookie, the saddest bracelet ever assembled.

"I MADE it," Wren says, very proud.

"It's beautiful," Madison says. And means it.

(She slips it on her wrist. It tickles. She does not take it off.)

And you two —" (Aiden and Chayten) "— same thing. Your mom is a friend of a friend. Did you know that, sweetheart? Half of you have no real stake in this place. Your moms don't lose ANYTHING if the actual family sells. You're guests."

He turns to the parents. "Maybe the blood family wants to talk to the guests about whether they really get to weigh in. Just an idea."

The parents look at each other. The chosen-family moms look at the blood-family moms. There is a silence Drystone is hoping to grow.

Aiden whispers: "Chayten. Are we GUESTS?"

Chayten goes still. So does Madison. So does Kellen.

Drystone is smiling.

P-552A Kid Takes It Apart

Carson is the one who moves first.

He doesn't say it loud. He says it the way he says things to Aiden when Aiden is scared.

He says, "You don't know what family is. You also don't know what belonging is."

Drystone says, "Excuse me?"

Carson says, "You looked at a property record. You think family is who's on a deed. You think belonging is who pays a mortgage. NONE of us own this place. NONE of our families own it. We're all renters. We rent the same condos every July. We have been renting them since before any of us were born. The cove doesn't belong to us. But WE belong to the cove. And that's something you can't put on a clipboard."

He walks down the line. He puts his hand briefly on each kid's shoulder as he passes.

"This is Reid. He's my cousin. We share a grandmother. Fine. But this is also Madison. She's not my cousin on paper. She's been my cousin every July since I was four. She taught me to swim. She made me cookies the summer my dog died. She is MY family. Not because of paper. Because she SHOWED UP."

He keeps walking.

"This is Chayten. Not blood. She braided my hair when I broke my arm. This is Kellen. Not blood. He carried me on his back when I sprained my ankle. This is Aiden. He is the LITTLEST of us. He is my COUSIN in EVERY WAY THAT MATTERS. Don't you stand there and tell us who our family is."

He turns back to Drystone. Fifteen years old. Much smaller than Drystone. Not afraid.

"We chose each other. They chose us. That's stronger than blood. It's stronger because we have to do it again every summer and we ALWAYS DO."

Madison steps up beside him. Then Reid. Then Ella. Blood-family kids and chosen-family kids tangled together, arms over shoulders, hands held.

Aiden: "We are NOT guests."

Pegasus, behind him, makes a sound very close to a unicorn cheering.

Drystone's smile dies. The kids' parents are now also stepping closer to each other. The chosen-family moms and the blood-family moms stand shoulder to shoulder.

Drystone has just cemented them.

Chapter 5 — The Showdown, Part 2

Beats 5 through 8, the C-130, and Drystone's defeat

BEAT 5 — KELLEN (main: requires the Stone)

Drystone tries again. "The bank offer STANDS. The family still can't AFFORD the geological work."

Kellen steps forward. He has been watching Drystone's posture all morning.

"Mr. Drystone. There's a piece of paper inside your jacket. You touch your lapel every two minutes. I've been counting. Will you take it out?"

"No."

"Okay."

He throws the stone. The stone skips off the wood of the dock. Bounces off a railing. Skips up the steps of Drystone's platform. Lands on Drystone's inside jacket pocket — through the FABRIC, somehow — and balances on the edge of a hidden fourth clipboard.

The clipboard tips out. Hits the wood. Falls open. Driedmore's internal memo about the bank. The lowballed appraisal. A list of three other small hotels Driedmore is doing the same thing to.

The kids' parents are now VERY interested. Drystone's face is the color of paper.

BEAT 5 — KELLEN (fallback: no Stone)

Kellen doesn't have the stone. He has the baseball that has been in his pocket since the third of July — the one he was taking shadow swings with when Drystone first arrived, the one he has spent the whole week imagining sending across the cove to the far-off island.

He pulls it out. He bounces it once on the dock — thock — and Drystone flinches. A small flinch. But Kellen sees it.

Kellen has been watching Drystone's posture all morning. He is twelve and he has been throwing things into and out of lakes for as long as he can remember; his eyes know what bodies do.

"Catch." He throws the baseball underhand. Drystone catches it. With his left hand. (He has been keeping his right hand free all morning to touch the inside lapel of his jacket.)

"Throw it back." Drystone does. Left hand again.

"Now throw it back with your right."

Drystone does. The thing inside his jacket falls slightly. The lapel shifts. The corner of a fourth clipboard becomes visible.

Madison's mother steps forward.

Kellen has not won the beat by throwing a magical stone. He has won it by understanding what a body looks like when it's hiding something, and by setting up the simplest possible test to expose it.

BEAT 6 — KEIRA (main: requires the Brush)

Drystone tries to recover. He says he doesn't know how those papers got in his pocket. He says someone must have PLANTED them. He says the kids are LIARS.

Keira pulls the Truth Brush from behind her ear. She holds it up to her eye. She looks through it at Drystone.

Drystone is bright YELLOW. His clipboards are YELLOW. His permits are YELLOW.

Madison's map glows BLUE.

Keira says, simply, "He's lying."

She says it small but everyone hears it.

A grown-up in the crowd says, "Wait — can you see something we can't?"

Keira says, "Yes. He's lying. I can see it."

The crowd believes her. They believe her because she's clearly telling the truth herself.

BEAT 6 — KEIRA (fallback: no Brush)

Keira doesn't have the brush. She has her sketchbook.

She has been drawing the whole time. The whole showdown. Since they walked down to the dock. She has not said a word. Nobody has noticed she's drawing — they've all been watching the kids who are talking.

She has filled six pages.

She walks up to the platform with her sketchbook open. She holds it up so the crowd can see. Then she flips through it, fast, like a flipbook.

The pages are Drystone. Drystone at the speech. Drystone when Madison started talking. Drystone when Chayten said listen. Drystone when the kids lined up. Drystone when he pulled out the bank offer. Drystone when he tried to peel the families apart. Drystone when Carson took it apart.

In every drawing, Drystone's left eye is narrower than his right. Every drawing. It is a tell.

"He does this when he's lying. Look."

The crowd looks at Drystone. Drystone is, at this exact moment, lying. The eye is doing it. Three different adults notice. Then five. Then everyone.

Keira hasn't won the beat with the truth brush. She has won it by drawing what was in front of her, carefully, the same way she always does.

BEAT 7 — ELLA (main: requires the Crown)

Drystone is purple in the face. He shouts, "I HAVE A PERMIT. THE PERMIT IS LEGAL. I AM PROCEEDING."

Ella steps in front of him. She is ten years old. She is smaller than him by half.

The invisible crown on her head becomes visible to him for one full second. He sees it. He doesn't know what he's seeing.

Ella does the thing Marigold taught her. She makes her face the deciding face.

She says, "I am Queen Ella of the Bright Water. And the answer is NO."

Drystone's mouth opens. His mouth closes. He cannot find a single word.

The crowd applauds. They don't see the crown. They see Ella's face, and they see Drystone's face, and they don't need to see the crown to know what just happened.

BEAT 7 — ELLA (fallback: no Crown)

Ella doesn't have a crown. She has lip gloss.

She pulls a small tube from her pocket. She also pulls out a small hand mirror. She does not explain why she has these. She just does. She applies the gloss carefully. She is ten years old and she does not stop applying her lip gloss to handle the most dangerous man on her dock.

The crowd, which had been expecting something dramatic, watches her put on lip gloss.

Drystone says, "Little girl, MOVE."

Ella, still working on her bottom lip in the mirror: "Just a second."

She finishes. She puts the cap back on. She puts the gloss in her pocket. She puts the mirror back. She looks up.

She walks directly toward Drystone. She does not stop two feet away. She walks until her toes are six inches from his shoes. She is exactly half his height. She has to tilt her head all the way back to look him in the face.

"No."

The lip gloss was the message. The message was: I am not afraid of you. I will finish my own business before I deal with yours.

Drystone has spent his career intimidating people who treated him as serious. He has no idea how to handle a ten-year-old who treats him as a chore.

He steps back. Ella's father, in the back, laughs out loud once and says, "That's my kid."

BEAT 8 — AIDEN ★ (main: requires Pegasus)

Drystone makes one last try. He is desperate now. He shouts, "I want PROOF this is REAL MAGIC. PROVE TO ME magic exists. Otherwise this is all a child's TANTRUM."

Aiden walks forward. He's been at the back the whole time, holding Pegasus's mane.

His BELIEF is what makes Pegasus visible to Drystone specifically. Adults can't normally see her. But when Aiden looks Drystone in the eye and BELIEVES — fully, without hedging, that there is a real unicorn standing on the dock — his belief is strong enough to make her real for Drystone. Just for Drystone. Just for that moment.

That is a seven-year-old's superpower. He believes harder than any adult ever could.

He whistles. Pegasus walks up the dock. Hooves clicking on the wood. Mane rainbow.

Aiden looks Drystone dead in the eye.

Drystone sees her. Just Drystone.

The other adults don't see her. But Drystone sees a real, talking, rainbow-maned unicorn standing three feet away from him, breathing steadily, looking at him with patient unicorn eyes.

His clipboards drop. His hard hat falls off. His face goes white.

Aiden says, "Mr. Drystone. This is Pegasus. She's a unicorn."

Pegasus says, "HI!"

She trots up to Drystone and sniffs him.

Drystone opens his mouth. He almost says "There's a unicorn." He stops. He realizes that if he says that, the other adults will think HE'S lost his mind.

He has to choose between admitting the magic and his career.

He chooses his career. He whispers, "I... can't... I can't put a unicorn on a permit."

Aiden says, "We know."

The crowd can't see what broke him, but they can see DRYSTONE — white-faced, sweating, beaten. He drops the offer letter on the dock. It flutters down.

The deal is dead.

Pegasus says, "LOOK AT ALL OF THEM!"

BEAT 8 — AIDEN (fallback: no Pegasus, very rare)

Aiden has no unicorn. He has the silver fish Chayten brought back for him from the Driftwood Stage.

He walks forward. The fish is in his pocket. He pulls it out and holds it up.

"Mr. Drystone. This is a fish from a place you've never been. My sister brought it back for me from a play she was in. The play was about a brother and a sister and how the sister never stopped looking for the brother. She got to be the sister in the play. And then she brought me this fish because she knows I like fishing. You don't have anyone who would do that for you. That's what's wrong with you."

The crowd goes very quiet.

A seven-year-old talking about love can stop a room faster than a unicorn can.

Drystone tries to recover. "...kid, you don't understand the situation —"

"I do understand. You don't have anyone who would bring you a fish. That's why you keep counting things. People who have someone who would bring them a fish don't have to count."

The crowd does not laugh. The crowd does not applaud. The crowd just listens to the small clear voice of the smallest kid on the dock telling the biggest grown-up the actual truth about him.

Drystone opens his mouth. Closes it. He has no answer to a seven-year-old's verdict on his entire life.

He sags.

P-595The C-130 Returns

The C-130 thunders overhead. Mid-week flyover.

Every adult and every kid on the dock looks up. The plane comes through the canyon huge and low. The dock vibrates. The windows of the bulldozer rattle. The trees on the bank shake.

Pegasus rears up onto her hind legs. She watches the plane. The plane is louder than she has ever heard anything be.

When it passes, Pegasus says, "ANOTHER one!"

Aiden says, "There's another one tomorrow."

Pegasus is delighted.

The kids' parents look at the kids. Then at the dock. Then back at the kids. They don't know what just happened. They know SOMETHING just happened.

Madison walks over to her parents.

She says, "We figured it out. The dock is going to be okay."

Her parents look at each other. They look at the dock. They want to ask. They don't know how.

P-596Drystone Breaks

Drystone gathers what's left of his clipboards. His tablet is dead.

He says, very quietly, "I'll be in my truck."

Nobody stops him. He walks down the dock road. He climbs into his truck. He sits in the driver's seat for ten minutes without starting the engine.

Eventually he drives away. He does not look back.

The Driedmore Data Center — West Cascadia Hub — does not break ground on July 4th. It does not break ground on July 5th. It will, several months from now, be quietly canceled.

The bank that made the rescue offer will be investigated, and the lake will not be drained, and the cove will stay a cove.

P-601The Dock Heals Overnight

For readers who earned all 8 items, played all 8 kids, and brought Pegasus through.

That night.

Sparklers and snap-its on the dock. Brisket gone. Lake dark. Stars coming up. The bats are out — one swoops just overhead to eat the mosquitoes and Aiden flinches and laughs at the same time.

Madison shares a cookie with Aiden. Aiden gives one to Pegasus, hidden in the sunroom (the unicorn is staying for the rest of the trip).

Reid is whittling. Kellen is throwing something into the lake. Keira is sketching the sunset. Ella is wearing her invisible crown. Carson is hyping a mini-golf rematch for tomorrow. Chayten is on the back dock with Aiden's fishing rod, casting in the dim light. Pickle is on her shoulder.

Across the lake, faintly, music is drifting from the campground. Tonight it's a guitar. Last night, in The Old Roar, it had been opera. Aiden hums along under his breath. He has been told to wait. He is waiting. He is also paying attention.

Reid walks over to the bank where the dock pilings meet the water. He plants his Driftwood staff at the base of the third piling. The staff sinks half its length into the soft earth. The wood at the base sends pale tendrils into the soil. The staff hums quietly. The water around it changes its flow — just a little, just enough.

He doesn't say anything. He walks back.

In the morning, the dock looks slightly better. Boards seem tighter. The third plank's gap is smaller. The water near the bank is no longer pulling dirt away. The pilings have settled.

The kids notice. The adults don't.

After the sparklers burn down, the eight kids walk to the end of the dock. They look at each other. Nobody has to say it.

They jump.

All eight, at the same time, in the same way they jumped on the first day of the trip. Splash. Eight at once. The sound is the same. The lake holds them up the same.

Pegasus, in the sunroom, can hear them. She makes a sound that is the unicorn version of good.

Aiden surfaces first. On his own. No hand reaching for Chayten's. No one waiting to grab the back of his suit. He spits water and laughs the way he laughed on the first day of the trip, when he jumped in by himself for the first time, when he was sure.

The others come up around him. Eight heads bobbing.

And then, just for a second — only Aiden sees this, and he won't tell anyone for years — there's a figure on the far shore of the cove. Standing on a rock that wasn't there a moment ago. Beard the color of cedar bark. Suspenders. Beat-up canvas hat pushed back on his head.

He waves.

Aiden waves back, one small hand up out of the water, his orange life jacket holding him bobbing.

The figure tips his hat. He turns. He walks into the trees on the far bank. The rock is gone.

Aiden doesn't say anything. He just holds the moment in his chest the way you hold a small bird you don't want to scare.

In the morning, the dock will be a little stronger. The lake will still be a lake. Drystone will be gone. The data center will be canceled. Sun Lakes will hold for another summer.

And another after that. And another. As long as the kids keep coming back.

That night.

Aiden tucks the moment away with all the other important things.

The Old Roar is still there. So is the cove. So are you.
