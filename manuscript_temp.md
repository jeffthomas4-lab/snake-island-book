# How to Read This Book

This is a *choose-your-own-adventure* book.

Start at **Passage 1** and read straight through until the Harbor Keeper sends the eight cousins off to their regions.

Then pick **one cousin** to follow first. Turn to the passage number listed for that cousin. Read their chapter. Inside the chapter there will be one bold choice with three options. Look for the **passage number** in the option you pick and turn to it.

When a cousin's chapter ends, the book will tell you what passages to turn to next. You can read the cousins in any order. When you have read as many as you want, turn to **Passage 400** to bring everyone home.

The **Showdown** is one long scene with eight beats, one per cousin. Each beat has two versions. Read the version that matches whether you turned to that cousin's chapter or not.

The book ends with one of two endings. You'll know which one when you get there.

## Passage 1 — The Synchronized Jump

![](images_small/jump.png)

The truck doors open at the same time.

It is the third of July. The drive from the western side of the state took most of the day. The last twenty minutes was the long winding downhill road, narrow, no shoulder, dropping into the canyon, and you'd miss the turn entirely except for the dented unmarked mailbox at the top of it. Every year somebody in the lead car panics that they've gone past. They haven't. There it is.

The dirt parking lot is at the bottom of the hill. Eight kids tumble out of four cars, into dust and dry grass and the smell of warm rock. Sun Lakes sits below them. The cove is the bluest it's ever been.

The grown-ups are still in the parking lot arguing about who made the best time. (The mom from Madison and Kellen's car insists she did. The dad from Carson and Kiera's car disagrees on principle. Uncle Bill, putting his boat in the water, is refereeing badly across the parking lot.) They are also arguing about what time to start the pizza for tonight. Pizza is the first-night dinner. It has been the first-night dinner for as long as any of them can remember. The argument isn't about whether, it's about *when* to put the first one in the oven, which depends on how hungry the kids are, which depends on how cold the lake is, which depends on a thousand things nobody is going to actually figure out before someone just goes ahead and turns on the oven.

The dock is still there. (Reid checks. He always checks. He noticed last summer that the third plank wobbles. This summer the wobble is a little worse. He doesn't tell anyone. He never does. He just files it away with everything else he notices and never mentions.)

The kids run. Not toward the condos. Not toward the bags. Toward the dock.

This is the rule. The rule is older than any of them. The rule has no rule-maker. It just is.

When you arrive at Sun Lakes, you put your bag down and you run to the dock and you all jump in at the same time.

Madison hits the dock first, because she's the oldest and her legs are longest. She kicks her sandals off as she runs and they land in the grass behind her. Carson is next, a half-step behind. Then Reid, then Chayten and Kellen at a near-tie, then Kiera (whose legs are catching up to the older kids each year and who is going to be faster than all of them in two more summers), then Ella in her bright pink swimsuit, then Aiden, last, the smallest, his bare feet slapping the wood.

This year is different for Aiden. He learned to swim this spring. *Really* learned. He spent six weekends at the local pool with his mom and now he can do an entire lap. He still wears his bright orange life jacket every time he hits the water, Mom's rule, and his rule too. Buckled tight at the chest, leg-strap clipped, the way she showed him. Last summer he was the kid the grown-ups had to bring in, careful, double-checked, with the jacket on backwards because nobody had shown him yet. This year he knows how to buckle it himself, and he is running to the end of the dock with the rest of them, and nobody is reaching for him because nobody has to. He is going to jump in with his cousins. By himself. For the first time. And nobody is going to ask if he's sure.

He is sure.

They line up at the end of the dock. Eight kids in a row. The wood is hot under their feet. The water is so clear they can see the brown shadow of the dock pilings underneath. A heron stops fishing in the shallows and looks up at them like *here we go again.*

Madison says, "Ready."

Reid says, "Ready."

Carson says, "READY."

Chayten says, "Ready."

Kellen says, "Ready."

Kiera says, "Ready."

Ella says, "Ready."

Aiden says, "Ready."

They count to three out loud, all together.

ONE.

TWO.

*THREE.*

Eight kids jump at once.

The splash is enormous. Eight bodies hit the water in the same second. Carson does a cannonball that is louder than physics allows. Madison goes in feet-first, ballet-clean. Reid hits the water like a tree falling. Chayten and Kellen go in side by side. Kiera does a half-twist she's been practicing. Ella jumps with her arms straight up and her crown of pink hair-ties intact. Aiden jumps with his eyes closed and his hand reaching for Chayten's, finding it under the water.

They surface together. Eight heads bobbing. Eight grins. The lake is exactly as cold as it always is, exactly as warm at the surface, exactly the right kind of *here* that means the trip has started.

Madison spits water. "We're here."

Aiden, breathing through his nose because his sister's hand is still around his and he refuses to let go, says, "*We're HERE.*"

The grown-ups are walking down the path now, carrying bags, still arguing about coolers and pizza times. They look up and they see eight kids in the water and they smile at each other in the specific way grown-ups smile when they have done one thing right.

Tonight is pizza night. The big house won't smell of brisket until tomorrow, or the night after, the dads' BBQ does its slow work for the middle of the trip (two of them tend it, the ones who care about brisket the way other people care about cars). But the porch and the side yard still hold the *ghost* of brisket from years past. The wood is soaked in it. The siding is. The cushions on the deck chairs are. Even after a year of rain and snow and canyon wind, the place still smells faintly of every brisket the dads have ever cooked here. Memory has a smell. So does Sun Lakes.

The bats are not yet out. They will be, tonight, after sunset, swooping low to eat the canyon mosquitoes. The wind is not yet howling. It will, after dark, when the canyon breathes the cold air down from Grand Coulee Dam.

But for now: just water. Just sun. Just eight kids in the cove on the third of July, exactly where they belong.

The summer has started.

The summer always starts this way.

## Passage 2 — Drystone Arrives

![](images_small/drystone.png)

It happens later in the afternoon.

The kids have done the things kids do at Sun Lakes in the first hours: unpacked into the same condos they always have (each family has the same condo every year; nobody decided this, it just is), grabbed snacks from the cooler, and scattered. The pizza dough is rising on the counter in the big house. The grown-ups are still in there laughing at something none of the kids will ever know.

Madison is reading on the grass with a tin of cookies beside her. She always brings cookies on the first day, or, some years, makes them during the trip. This was a bring-them year. She made them last night. They are chocolate-chip and they have a small amount of cinnamon in them that nobody but Aiden ever names.

Chayten is paddle-boarding back from the back dock with Aiden, who is sitting cross-legged on the front of the board holding a fishing rod and talking to it.

Reid is at the edge of the main dock, whittling a stick.

Kellen is in the grass taking shadow swings with a baseball bat. He is not hitting anything. He is doing the thing where you look out at the far-off island on the other side of the lake and you visualize the perfect arc, pitch in, weight back, hips through, contact, follow-through, the ball sailing all the way across the cove and landing on that island. He has hit that island in his head about forty times today. He is going to keep hitting it.

Carson is on the grass with a putter, hyping a mini-golf game for later in the week, a morning round before the heat beats down.

Kiera is sketching the lake in a notebook with a small soft pencil.

Ella is sitting on the porch with her chin on her hands. She is just sitting. She is not putting on makeup, not playing a game, not doing anything in particular. She is *with her cousins.* All week. No school. No one telling her what's next. She is thinking about exactly nothing, and it is the best feeling she has had all year.

Aiden is, as established, talking to his fishing rod. ("Wait. *Wait.* Fish are in WATER. How am I going to fish? I can't fish from the dock. I have to be IN the water. But if I'm in the water I'll scare the fish. This is, Chayten. CHAYTEN. How do people FISH.")

The man arrives in a white pickup truck.

He pulls into the dirt parking lot. He gets out. He is wearing a hard hat. In a wide open dirt parking lot. That is the first weird thing. He has three clipboards under one arm and a tablet in his other hand. He walks down to the dock without looking up from the tablet.

Carson sees him first.

Carson says, "Uh."

The man does not introduce himself. He pulls out a sign on a metal stake and hammers the stake into the grass at the head of the dock. He uses a small mallet. He hits the stake four times. He does not look up.

The sign reads:

> COMING SOON
> DRIEDMORE DATA CENTER, WEST CASCADIA HUB
> GROUNDBREAKING JULY 4, 12:00 NOON
> THIS COVE IS UNDER CONTRACT TO DRIEDMORE PROPERTIES

Madison sets her book down. She walks over. She reads it twice. She knows what a data center is. She knows what they need.

Carson asks. Madison says, "They use a LOT of water. Cooling. They want to drain the lake."

Aiden, paddle-boarding back, says, "But we can't have a lake if there's no water."

Madison says, "That's the whole point."

The man finally looks up. He sees a small group of kids standing around his sign. He sighs.

"Don't TOUCH the sign, kids. This here is a malfeasance of zoning. I am Mr. Drystone of Driedmore Properties." He pronounces *ecosystem* "echo-system" without noticing.

Reid says, quietly, "My family has come here for years. Why are the other people selling?"

Drystone laughs. "Selling? Sweetheart, the people who own this place have a dock with NO foundation. The water's eaten the ground out from under it. They can't AFFORD the geological work. Driedmore is RESCUING them. We're the good guys here."

(The rental families who come every summer, Madison's family, Reid's, Chayten's, Carson's, Kiera's, Ella's, Aiden's, Kellen's, don't own a square inch of Sun Lakes. They have rented the condos every July for as long as the kids have been alive. The cove belongs to the family who runs it. The rental families just *belong* to the cove. There's a difference. There's not really a difference.)

He scans something on his tablet. He does not look at any of the kids' faces.

He leaves.

## Passage 3 — The Kids Can't Fix This

The kids stand around the sign for a long minute.

Reid looks at the dock. The third plank is wobbling under his weight more than it did even last summer. He bends down. He looks at the pilings underneath. He can see, in the clear water, the way the dirt around them is being pulled by the current. The dock foundation IS being eroded. Drystone is right about that part.

The kids try the obvious things.

Madison wants to find the adults. The adults are inside, finishing the lunch dishes and starting prep for tonight's brisket. Uncle Bill is out back, telling somebody a long story about the boat. Madison does not interrupt him. She also realizes, as she's standing outside the door, that her own mother has been talking about "the situation" all week on the phone. The grown-ups already KNOW about Driedmore. They have been talking about it for weeks. They have been quietly considering it because of the dock.

Reid suggests pulling the sign down. Carson says no, that's not how you win. That just makes them put up another sign and now you also have a vandalism charge.

Kellen suggests calling somebody. Kiera points out they don't know who.

Aiden, very quietly, says, "Can we just ask the lake to stop it?"

There is a pause. Several of the older kids laugh, but the laugh is not unkind. Madison doesn't laugh at all. She looks at Aiden. Then she looks past him.

She looks at the sunroom.

The door is half-open. Inside, the rafts are piled up against the wall, drying from the arrival-jump a few hours ago. The big inflatable unicorn is on top of the pile.

The unicorn is bobbing.

There's no wind.

## Passage 4 — Drift to the Sunroom

![](images_small/sunroom.png)

The kids drift toward the main house.

The sunroom door is half-open. The big inflated unicorn is still bobbing.

Aiden walks in first. Aiden is seven and has not yet learned to be afraid of weird things.

The other seven follow.

The sunroom is warm, warmer than the rest of the house. The sun has been on it all afternoon and the wall of windows has trapped the heat. The plastic of the inflatables is warm against the skin. The smell is the specific smell of Sun Lakes in July: the deep-soaked brisket-memory of the side yard, sunscreen on the kids' shoulders, lake water still on their suits, warm plastic, the faint sourdough smell of pizza dough rising in the kitchen, and one other smell that nobody can name, cold and wet and faintly green, like cave water on a hot day, even though there is no cave water in the sunroom.

Madison says, "Is that smell new?"

Reid says, "It feels new."

Chayten sets the paddle board against the wall. Kellen leans the baseball bat against the doorframe. Carson pockets his putter. Kiera closes her sketchbook. Ella stands up from where she's been sitting.

Eight kids are now standing in the sunroom, surrounded by inflatables, with no clear plan.

Aiden walks up to the unicorn raft. He places one small hand on its plastic side.

The unicorn raft is warm.

The unicorn raft tilts its big inflatable head toward him.

Aiden does not flinch. He just nods, like the unicorn raft has confirmed something he already suspected.

Outside, somewhere up the canyon, a sound begins. It is far off and getting closer. The sound is enormous and low and the kids feel it in their chests before their ears catch it.

The C-130.

It comes through the canyon a couple of times during their week here. Not every day. Just a handful of times during the trip, the giant gray cargo plane swinging low between the cliffs because the canyon is the perfect training run. The kids look forward to it. The grown-ups also look forward to it. The grown-ups pretend they don't, but they do, every single one of them. When somebody yells *PLANE!* every kid in earshot drops what they're doing and runs to the deck to watch, and every grown-up in earshot drifts to the same deck a few seconds later, all of them with the same dumb happy face when the plane comes around the bend.

But today is different. Today the sunroom is *humming.*

## Passage 5 — The C-130

![](images_small/c130.png)

The C-130 thunders past.

It is so loud the windows rattle. The frame of the sunroom shakes. The pile of inflatables shifts. Aiden grabs Chayten's hand. Reid grins. Carson covers his ears and laughs. Kiera instinctively shields her sketchbook even though it's already closed. Ella's hand mirror falls off the windowsill and onto the carpet without breaking.

The plane is huge. Through the sunroom windows, the kids can see it for a full second, gray and low and almost stupidly close to the cliffs, and then it is past, the sound trailing behind it as it disappears around the bend.

The sunroom is still humming.

The pile of inflatables is no longer a pile.

The rafts have moved.

The unicorn raft is on the floor in front of Aiden. The canoe-shaped raft is in front of Madison. A long log-shaped raft is in front of Reid. A sturdy wheeled-boat raft is in front of Carson. A racing canoe is in front of Kellen. A flat painter's raft is in front of Kiera. A small boat with golden trim is in front of Ella. A driftwood-bowed boat is in front of Chayten.

Each kid is standing in front of a raft.

Each raft was waiting for them.

Nobody says anything for a moment.

Then Aiden climbs onto the unicorn raft.

He doesn't think about it. He just does.

The unicorn raft lifts off the floor.

The walls of the sunroom go away.

The lake outside is no longer the lake outside. There is a bigger water. A *louder* water. Madison can hear it, a big sound, far off, like a waterfall. Like the BIGGEST waterfall.

Each raft starts to slide off in a different direction. The kids try to call to each other but the wind is loud. Aiden, on his unicorn, is laughing. Madison, in her canoe, is shouting instructions. Reid is calmly steering. Carson is whooping. Kellen is grinning. Kiera is open-mouthed. Ella is sitting perfectly straight with her hands folded in her lap as if she has been waiting for a royal carriage and one has finally arrived.

The last thing each kid hears is Madison's voice, far off: "WHATEVER YOU DO, STAY ON YOUR RAFT UNTIL IT LANDS."

(Madison is the oldest. She always knows.)

The rafts pull them through. The sunroom is far behind them. The bigger water is below them. The C-130 is somewhere far away in a world they have just, briefly, left.

## Passage 10 — Eight Rafts Land in the Meadow

The rafts land together.

Not where they were heading separately, somewhere new. A green meadow under a sky that is the color of green water seen from underneath. The grass is the brightest green any of the kids have ever seen. The air smells like rain that has not happened.

They climb off their rafts. They stand in a loose group. They check that everyone is here. Madison counts heads. Eight. Good.

A roaring sound is filling the meadow from every direction. It is the sound Madison heard from her canoe, bigger, louder, more present. It is a waterfall. It is *every* waterfall.

In front of them, where the meadow falls away, they can see why.

Dry Falls is wet.

Dry Falls, the enormous cliff at the head of the canyon, the one that is in every school field trip the kids have ever taken in this part of the state, the one that has been a *dry* falls for fifteen thousand years, is *roaring.* Water coming over the lip in a sheet a thousand feet wide. The sound is the sound of geology happening.

Aiden whispers, "*Oh.*"

Carson whispers, "*Whoa.*"

Madison says, "Where are we?"

A voice from behind them, warm and low and amused, says, "Lake family. *Good.* Been too quiet around here."

The eight kids turn.

## Passage 11 — The Harbor Keeper

![](images_small/harbor_keeper.png)

He is sitting on a log at the edge of the meadow.

He is large. Not big-as-a-tree-being large, not big-as-a-mammoth large. Just *human* large, broad shouldered, a little stooped, the size of a grown-up uncle who has spent a lifetime carrying things. He has a beard the color of cedar bark. He is wearing what looks like a faded denim shirt and suspenders. He has work boots. He has a beat-up canvas hat pushed back on his head.

He is whittling.

A small pile of wood shavings has gathered at his feet.

He looks up as the kids turn.

He smiles.

The smile is *specifically familiar.*

Reid notices it first, in the part of his brain that notices things without telling anyone. The smile is Uncle Bill's smile. The exact one Uncle Bill uses when he is about to give them a small job to do and is going to enjoy watching them do it. Reid does not say so out loud. He just keeps watching the Harbor Keeper's hands.

The man says, "Sit down, sit down. Don't stand around looking like you got off a bus."

He gestures to a half-circle of flat stones around the log. They are the perfect height for sitting. They were not there a second ago.

The kids sit.

The man keeps whittling. He does not introduce himself for a while. He just works. The shavings curl off his knife in long thin spirals. Aiden watches with the kind of attention Aiden gives to things that are mysterious and excellent.

After a long minute, the man says, "You can call me the Harbor Keeper. That's the name I have on this side. I have had others. Last visitor I had, a long time ago, called me something else. I don't remember what. I have a bad memory for what people call me. I have a *good* memory for the people themselves."

Madison says, "What... what is this place?"

The Harbor Keeper looks up. "You're in The Old Roar. That's the name for what's underneath your cove. Underneath every place that gets loved enough, long enough, by the same families coming back year after year. The Old Roar is the memory of the land. It is the *attention* the land remembers being given. Your families have been giving this lake attention for a long time. The lake remembers. So do I."

He puts the whittling down. He looks at each of the kids in turn.

He says, "Madison. Carson. Chayten. Reid. Kellen. Kiera. Ella. Aiden."

Nobody told him their names.

The kids do not react the way kids in movies do. They do not gasp. They just sit there and absorb it. Aiden, characteristically, just nods like he already suspected.

The Harbor Keeper says, "There is a problem at your dock. I know about it. The lake knows about it. The Old Roar is connected to your cove and what happens up there happens down here. If your lake gets drained, this world ends. So. We need to fix it."

He stands up. He stretches. His knees crack like old wood.

"Each of you can earn something here. Something that will help up there. There are eight things to find, one per kid. None of you can earn another kid's thing. Each one is shaped for you specifically. The Old Roar already knows what you need. You'll find it because the world will lead you to it."

He turns and gestures across the meadow. The grass behind him opens out into eight different regions, visible all at once, a quiet pool, a driftwood stage, a wide grassy plain, a rocky woodland, a long flat lake, a basalt cliff, a moss circle, a meadow with a sideways-growing rainbow.

The Harbor Keeper says, "Each region is shaped for one of you. The Pool is for Madison. The Plains are for Carson. The Wood is for Reid. The Stage is for Chayten. The Skipping Lake is for Kellen. The Painted Cliff is for Kiera. The Bright Water Court is for Ella. The Meadow is for Aiden."

He looks at each kid as he says their name. The grass paths to the eight regions glow, faintly, one at a time.

"You'll each go to your own. The world will lead you. When you've earned what you came for, the way home will find you. We'll all meet back here when it's time."

He picks his whittling back up. He gives them a small nod.

"Off you go, then. The lake is waiting."

## Passage 110 — Arrival at the Quiet Pool

![](images_small/madison_region.png)

Madison's raft slides to a stop on the shore of a pool so still you could roll a coin across it.

The raft is a wooden canoe now, with her name carved into the side: **MADISON**. The tin of cookies she was holding in the sunroom is still in her lap. None of them are crushed. (She checks.)

The pool is shaped like a heart, if a heart had been drawn by someone who'd never seen one. Rocks at the edge. Cottonwoods leaning over the water. A heron standing on one leg in the shallows, looking at her like she's late.

She's alone. The other seven landed somewhere else. The silence is bigger than she expected. She steadies herself. Oldest kid. She knows what she's doing.

Three heads pop up out of the water.

Three girls. One her age. One younger. One small.

Their hair drips upward instead of down.

The oldest one tilts her head. "Are you the cookie-bringer?"

The middle one says, "She IS the cookie-bringer."

The youngest one is already chewing on something.

The oldest climbs out. Her feet leave wet prints that turn into flowers. "I'm Pip. That one's Lark. The one chewing is Wren. Wren, what are you eating."

Wren opens both hands. They're full of pondweed she's mashed into the shape of a cookie. It is the saddest cookie Madison has ever seen.

"It's my OWN recipe," Wren says.

"It's TERRIBLE," Pip says.

Madison opens her tin. The cookies inside smell like brown sugar and brisket smoke and home.

She is about to share them.

Then Lark says: "Wait. First, sit down."

## Passage 111 — The Cared-for Moment

![](images_small/lake_sisters.png)

"Wait. Don't sit. STAND. We have been watching you walk down the bank."

Madison blinks. "Why?"

Pip steps closer. "You're a DANCER. We can tell from how you walk. The way your feet land. The way your spine moves with your head. We have been HOPING for a dancer for a very long time."

Lark says, "We knew one of you would be. Pools know. We have been WAITING."

Madison is a little surprised. She knows she's a good dancer, her teacher has told her, her family has told her, she has the trophies and the placements in her room that say so. But she has never met an audience this hungry for a dancer. The Sisters are looking at her the way people look at something they've been hoping to see for years.

Wren tugs her hand. "Can we see you do something? Please? We've been thinking about it for so long."

Pip raises one small wet hand. Three flat stones lift from the pool and hover an inch above the surface, forming a small triangular stage just within Madison's reach. The water around them goes glassy.

Madison thinks for one second. Then she picks the adagio, the one her teacher built FOR her last semester. The hardest one she does in studio. The long arabesque. The turn that took her three months to clean.

She steps onto the first stone. Her body knows what to do.

She dances three measures. They are very good measures. The arabesque is exactly as long as it should be. The turn lands clean. She has danced this same sequence a hundred times. She has never danced it with this much attention from an audience.

The three Sisters do not clap. They do not whistle. They WATCH the way an audience that has been waiting for years watches when the wait is finally over, with their full faces, with their breath held, with the quiet that says *please don't let this end*.

When she steps off the last stone, the pool ripples in a pattern around the three stones that looks, faintly, like a standing ovation made of water.

Pip whispers: "Thank you, Madison. That was BEAUTIFUL."

Lark wipes a Sister-tear that goes up. "We don't get dancers. People don't dance for a pool. They dance for other people. You danced for us anyway."

Madison feels something specific in her chest. Not surprise, she knew she could do it. Not the warmth of being praised, she gets praised, that's familiar. Something newer than that: the warmth of having SHARED something she loves with an audience that genuinely, specifically needed to see it. Her grace went somewhere today that it has never gone before. Three small water-girls had been waiting for years for it. She brought it.

She sits down on the bank. Her hair drips upward, just a little. She catches sight of herself in the pool's reflection, fifteen years old, breath steady, all three Sisters still beaming at her, and laughs out loud. The laugh of someone who brought a gift and discovered the gift was exactly the right one.

Pip puts a small wet hand on Madison's knee. "And one more thing, before you go. You are Madison the Dancer, who shares her grace with audiences who deserve to see it. The pool will remember that name. You should too."

Pip nods. "Go back to your cousins now. We will remember today for a very long time. You were exactly what we had been hoping for."

Madison laughs. It's a real laugh. The kind she usually saves for inside her own head.

The Sisters wait. They've done their part. The rest is hers.

Pip says, "We have something to tell you. About the lake-up-above. About the man with the clipboards."

Madison sits forward.

She is, in fact, ready now.

## Passage 112 — Madison's Choice

Pip tells her the rest fast.

The Lake Sisters have been watching the lake-up-above for years. They've seen Mr. Drystone's boats. They've seen him drop papers into the water, papers they were never meant to read. The papers sank. The Sisters caught them. They have them now.

But the papers are in three pieces, and only one piece is here at the pool. The other two are elsewhere.

Pip is holding the first piece. It glints in her hand.

"How do you want to do this?" Pip asks.

Madison thinks for a second.

## Passage 113 — Share the Cookies, Take the Piece
Madison opens her tin and shares the cookies fairly. One for Pip. One for Lark. The biggest one for Wren, because Wren is smallest.

(Wren cries with joy. Lark and Pip do not comment. They've seen Wren cry over a particularly good rock.)

The Sisters eat their cookies the way creatures eat who have never had anything baked before. Pip nibbles. Lark inhales hers in two bites. Wren tries to make her cookie last by licking it.

When they're done, Pip looks at her sisters. The three of them nod at each other in the way of people who have just received something and now have to give something back. (That's how exchanges work in the Old Roar. You bring something. They bring something. That is the whole agreement.)

Lark slips back into the pool and comes up holding a small, perfectly round, lavender-colored stone. It is warm in her hand. It is glowing very faintly, the way a smooth river rock looks when a kid pulls it out of shallow water and holds it up to the sun.

"For you," she says. "It's a *quiet stone.* Hold it when a place is too loud. It pulls the noise into itself and gives you back about ten seconds of quiet. Sisters made it. It only works if you got it from a sister."

Madison closes her hand around it. It is exactly the kind of thing she did not know she needed and has needed her whole life.

Wren hands her something next. It is a small pondweed-and-pebble bracelet she has clearly been making during the cookie sharing. It is, like Wren's cookie, the saddest bracelet ever assembled.

"I MADE it," Wren says, very proud.

"It's beautiful," Madison says. And means it.

(She slips it on her wrist. It tickles. She does not take it off.)

Then Pip hands Madison the paper.

It is a single sheet, water-warped at the edges, the ink swimming a little but readable. It shows the cove. It shows the data center building Drystone plans to put on the dry lakebed once the water is gone. It shows the drainage path, straight down the canyon, into the river system, away forever.

It does NOT show the bank fraud. It does NOT show the lowballed appraisal. Those pieces are elsewhere.

Madison rolls the paper carefully and tucks it inside her cookie tin. The tin clicks closed. It is warm in her hands.

"This is most of it," Pip says. "Not all. But you'll know what to say."

Madison nods.

*If the reader has already played Aiden's branch and unlocked Pegasus, a small rainbow arcs over the pool for no reason. Lark looks up. "Huh," she says. "That hasn't happened before." Madison doesn't know what it means yet. She will.*

Wren tugs her hand. "Will you come back?"

Madison kneels so she's eye-level with the smallest Sister. Wren's eyes are the color of pool water in the morning.

"Yes," Madison says.

She means it.

The Sisters walk her back toward the meadow. Pip stops at the edge of the trees. "Tell our older sister hi."

Madison says, "Who's your older sister?"

Lark says, "The big one. The one your hotel is named for. The cove."

Madison stops walking.

The lake at home. *Their* lake. The one she's been swimming in every July of her life. The one her cousins all swim in. The one Aiden took his first dip in last summer with his life jacket on backwards.

That lake is family to these girls.

That lake is family to HER.

She feels something land in her chest that she's been almost-knowing for years.

She walks back to her canoe with the Map in her tin and a feeling in her chest she'll figure out later.

## Passage 114 — Ask What They Need First
Madison doesn't open the tin yet.

She looks at Pip and says, "What do you NEED?"

Pip blinks. Lark blinks. Wren stops chewing.

"Most people," Pip says slowly, "don't ask us that."

Lark adds, "Most people are HUNGRY for what we have. They don't notice we might need something too."

Madison waits.

The Sisters look at each other in the way sisters look at each other.

Pip says, "The other two pieces of the map. We can't reach them."

The first piece, the one Pip is holding, sank near the pool, easy to catch.

The other two sank farther downstream. Out past where the pool turns into a slow river. Out where a grumpy old beaver named **Branch** has been collecting interesting things in his lodge for two hundred years. Branch caught the other two pieces and he will not give them back. He says the river is his and what lands in the river is his.

The Sisters have asked him a hundred times. He always says no.

"He might say yes to YOU," Pip says. "He likes people who SIT."

Madison thinks about her own grandmother. Her grandmother also liked people who sit.

Madison says, "Take me to him."

The Sisters slip into the water. Madison walks along the bank. Lark pops up every few steps to point. The pool narrows. The water starts moving. Cottonwood leaves spin past on the current.

At a bend in the river, a beaver lodge the size of a small cabin rises out of the water. Smoke comes out of a hole in the top. The smoke smells like dried mint.

Madison knocks on a stick.

A grumpy face appears. **Branch**. He has a small pair of spectacles on the end of his nose and a tea towel over one shoulder.

"What."

Madison says, "Hello, Branch. I'm Madison. May I come in?"

Branch is suspicious. But Madison didn't lead with what she wanted. Branch finds this confusing in a way he likes.

"...Fine."

Inside the lodge: a tiny rocking chair, a tea kettle on a stone fire, and a wall of WONDERFUL trash. A spoon. A fishing lure. A pair of sunglasses. A small ceramic pig. Two folded pieces of paper that Madison's eyes go to immediately and then, very deliberately, away from.

![](images_small/branch_beaver.png)

Branch pours her a thimble of tea. (For a beaver, a thimble is a normal-sized cup. For Madison, it is approximately one drop.)

She sits.

She sips her one drop of tea.

She does not ask about the papers.

She asks about the spoon.

Branch lights up. He tells her about the spoon. The spoon takes nine minutes. Then he tells her about the ceramic pig. The pig takes longer.

Forty minutes go by. (Time in The Old Roar is slower, but still, forty minutes is forty minutes. The Harbor Keeper said *time for four*. She's spending it.)

At the end of his stories, Branch is quiet for a long time. Then he says, "You're the first person in two hundred years who didn't ask me for the papers first."

Madison says, "I figured you'd tell me about them when you wanted to."

Branch hands her the two folded papers without ceremony.

Then he says, "I'm coming with you."

Madison says, "To the lake-up-above?"

"To wherever the man with the clipboards is," Branch says. "I'll witness."

She rolls the three pieces of the Map together. Now it shows everything: the drainage, the data center, the bank fraud, the lowballed appraisal, the wrong permit Drystone filed. The whole shape of the lie.

Branch waddles to the door, picks up a small bow tie from his wall of trash, and clips it on.

"Ready," he says.

## Passage 115 — Notice Wren First
Madison was about to open her tin.

She doesn't.

Because Wren has gone quiet.

The smallest Sister was chewing her pondweed cookie a second ago. Now she's holding it in both hands and staring at the pool.

Madison kneels. "Wren. Hey. What's the matter?"

Pip and Lark go still.

Wren says, in a voice so small Madison has to lean in: "She didn't sing last night."

Madison says, "Who didn't?"

Pip says, very quickly, "Nobody. It's nothing."

Lark says, "Pip."

Pip closes her mouth.

Wren says, "The lady. The one who sings."

Madison sits down on the bank. The wood is warm. "Tell me."

The Sisters look at each other. Then Pip nods.

Lark sings, very quietly, three notes. The pool hums them back. The three notes are beautiful, clear, full, the kind of voice that carries.

"That's HER voice," Wren says. "She sings here some nights. Not all. Some. She's been singing here for as long as we've been here. We don't know who she is. The lake remembers her. We listen for her."

Madison's skin prickles.

Because Madison knows that voice.

She doesn't know HOW she knows it. She just does. Like the way she knows the smell of brisket smoke. Like the way she knows the sound of the C-130 a full second before it shows up over the canyon.

She HAS heard this voice. Two summers ago. A hot July night, late, after the brisket was gone and the kids were supposed to be asleep. The sound came across the water from somewhere nobody could place, somebody, somewhere, singing opera. Big, beautiful, impossible. The grown-ups had laughed about it the next morning. They'd all pretended not to know who it was. The mystery had become a family joke.

But Madison had watched her mother's face that next morning. Her mother had been very busy looking at her coffee.

Madison feels her chest do a thing.

She doesn't say anything yet.

She says: "Why didn't she sing last night?"

Pip says, "We don't know. She comes when she comes. When she doesn't, the lake is quieter than it should be. Wren feels it most."

Wren is crying without making a sound. The tears go up her face instead of down.

Madison does the thing she does best in the whole world.

She puts an arm around the smallest Sister. She doesn't say *it'll be okay*. She doesn't say *don't worry*. She just holds her and lets her cry the wrong direction.

After a while, Pip says quietly, "There's another piece of the map."

Madison says, "I thought there were three."

Pip says, "There are. But there's also a fourth. It's not on paper. It's a song. The song the lady sings. If you can hear it, you'll know what the lake itself thinks about Mr. Drystone. The lake has an opinion."

Lark says, "It's not loud. But it's real."

Madison thinks for a second.

Then she does the only thing she can do, the thing she's actually good at.

Pip seems to know. She lifts a hand. Three flat stones rise up out of the pool, one after another, arranged in a line across the water. They are perfectly still. They are also, Madison notices, the same color as the basalt at home.

Madison steps onto the first one. It holds.

She knows the count before her head does. She has danced this same sequence a hundred times this spring; her body falls into it the way an old book falls open at the right page.

She steps to the second stone in one clean line, the way her old teacher used to drill it: head up, shoulders down, the foot finding the place before you trust it. She turns once on the second stone. She lands.

She crosses to the third stone and finishes in fourth position, arms soft, eyes closed.

She holds it.

The pool hums.

Three notes. Low. Then a long pause.

And then, from somewhere far away, maybe under the pool, maybe somewhere on the other side of the canyon, three notes come back. Sung in answer. The voice is a woman's. Operatic. Big. Beautiful. She is singing in The Old Roar tonight after all. She is answering Madison's dance the way a singer answers a question.

Wren laughs through her wrong-direction tears.

Pip presses both hands to her mouth.

Lark just listens.

The lady's song goes on for a long minute. None of them speak. Madison's eyes are wet too, though she couldn't tell you why.

When the song ends, Pip hands Madison the first piece of the map. Then she dives into the pool and comes up holding the second piece, then the third. They were here all along. The Sisters had been waiting to see if she was the right person to give them to.

She is.

The three pieces fit together cleanly. The whole Map. Everything Drystone has done, everything he plans.

"And the song?" Madison asks.

Pip says, "You'll carry it. The lake will remember that you heard."

Madison rolls the Map. She tucks it in her tin.

The tin glows, very faintly, like firelight inside a paper lantern.

She doesn't know what she's going to tell her mom about this later.

She'll figure it out.

## Passage 130 — Arrival on the Coulee Plains

Carson's raft slides to a stop on a wide grass plain that goes farther than his eyes can follow. The raft has become a sturdy wooden boat on tiny wheels. He hops out. The grass is the bright kind, the kind that only grows when there's been a lot of rain.

Big Coulee is already telling him.

She doesn't stop for breath.

## Passage 131 — The Go-hard Moment

![](images_small/carson_region.png)

Big Coulee explains. The Long Race is in thirty minutes. She has been training for six moons. Her babies keep wandering off. She is NERVOUS. She needs a worthy opponent.

"WILL YOU RACE ME?"

Carson is about to say yes when Big Coulee does something he doesn't expect.

She rams him.

Not hard. Not anywhere near hard. A nudge with her flank, the kind of nudge a wooly mammoth might give a tree she's not sure is in her way. Carson is a hundred and twenty pounds and Big Coulee is roughly ten thousand. He goes sprawling. Lands in the grass. Laughing.

"GET UP," Big Coulee bellows, cheerfully. "RAM ME BACK."

Carson stands up. He looks at Big Coulee. Then he runs at her, shoulder lowered. He puts his entire body into the shove. He hits her side at full speed.

She doesn't move. Her flower crown slips slightly.

She *cheers.*

"YES. YES. THAT is what I am TALKING about, child. Do you SEE? You can't hurt me. You couldn't hurt me if you ran at me with a TRUCK. Stop holding YOURSELF in. I have raced thirty competitors in six moons and every one of them softened it. Every one of them. They thought I would be sad if I lost. Do you know what that's like?"

Carson does, in fact, know what that's like.

"Today you race like you mean it. Like a champion. Not like an older cousin. Race ME. You can be polite tomorrow."

She straightens up. "*EXCELLENT.* Then we have a RACE. Choose your path."

## Passage 133 — Race Straight
Carson grins. "Just race. Straight to the finish line. Fastest wins."

Big Coulee's eyes light up. She rears back onto her hind legs (a thing that should not be physically possible for a wooly mammoth and yet) and trumpets so loud the grass flattens for ten feet around her.

"YES."

The bird on her ear (her name is Pip-Pip; she is small and self-important and carries a tiny clipboard) flutters over and explains the rules. Two flagged poles a quarter-mile apart. Around the far pole. Back. First nose across the line wins.

Carson stretches. Big Coulee stretches. (Big Coulee's stretches involve her entire body and look like a yoga teacher trying to invent a new pose.)

Pip-Pip raises a tiny flag.

Lowers it.

"GO."

Carson runs. He hasn't run this hard since the eighth-grade two-mile time trial. His lungs are burning before he gets halfway. Big Coulee is way ahead of him, thundering, narrating: "AND BIG COULEE TAKES the EARLY LEAD, child! She is APPROACHING the far pole! She is ROUNDING it!"

He keeps going. He's not going to win. He knows he's not going to win.

He's having the time of his life.

He rounds the far pole maybe twenty seconds behind her. She is already a quarter of the way back, her trunk pumping like a metronome, the flower crown bouncing on her head.

He sprints anyway. Full effort. No backing off.

Big Coulee crosses the line first. She rears back again, trumpets, and starts a victory lap before Carson is even close.

He finishes ten seconds behind her, gasping, hands on his knees.

Big Coulee thunders back to him. She is delighted. She picks him up in her trunk and sets him on her back, where he sits sideways and laughs because he is too tired to do anything else.

"CHILD," she says. "You ran like you MEANT it. That is rare and that is GOOD."

Pip-Pip presents the prize from her clipboard: a small, smooth piece of polished mammoth tusk on a leather cord. The Tusk-Charm. "Carved from my own great-grandmother's tusk," Big Coulee says. "Whoever wears this, when you stand somewhere and ask people to stand with you, they will. It works because they already wanted to. The charm just makes the asking visible."

Carson loops the cord around his neck. The charm is warm against his collarbone.

*If the reader has played Madison's branch, a small spotted otter comes up to the finish line carrying a cookie. "From the cookie-bringer," she says, and waddles off before Carson can ask who that is.*

She walks him back to his boat-on-wheels. Tuft and Tumble follow her, occasionally falling over and getting back up.

"Come back next year," Big Coulee says. "I'll be in better shape. So will you. We'll do this properly."

Carson says, "Deal."

## Passage 134 — Race and Gather
Carson looks at Tuft and Tumble, who are currently chewing on each other's ears.

"Race AND we gather babies along the way," he says. "Whoever gets back to the finish line with the most babies wins."

Big Coulee freezes.

Then she lets out a sound that is half-trumpet, half-laugh, half-cry. (Mammoths can have three halves of a sound. It's a mammoth thing.)

"CHILD," she says. "That is the BEST rule anyone has ever made. We use it FOREVER. Pip-Pip, write that down."

Pip-Pip, scandalized, writes it down on her clipboard.

The catch: Tuft and Tumble are not the only baby mammoths in The Old Roar. There are five more, scattered across the plain, all under three feet tall, all extremely lost. The race is on. The first to thirty babies (or to be more accurate, the first to all seven) wins.

Pip-Pip drops the flag.

Carson sprints. Big Coulee thunders. They peel off in different directions.

Carson finds the first baby in a patch of wildflowers, a small mammoth so covered in pollen she sneezes when he picks her up. He tucks her under his arm. Two more are stuck in a bramble together. He frees them. They cling to his legs and waddle behind him.

By the time he reaches the far pole, he has four babies, one under each arm, two clinging to his back. He turns. Big Coulee is coming the other way with three babies on her broad back and one in her trunk.

She sees him. She brakes hard. She trumpets, "CHILD. YOU ARE TIED WITH ME."

Carson is laughing too hard to answer.

The last baby, the smallest, no bigger than a loaf of bread, is sitting in the middle of the field, very confused, eating a flower. Carson and Big Coulee both stop.

They look at each other.

"It would be a TIE," Carson says.

Big Coulee narrows her eyes. "I have NEVER tied a race."

"I have NEVER won a race against a mammoth."

They look at the baby.

The baby eats another flower.

They both run for it. They reach it at the same second. Big Coulee scoops the baby with her trunk. Carson takes its other end. They cross the finish line together, holding the same baby, with eleven other babies clinging to them.

A tie. Pip-Pip's tiny clipboard explodes. (Not literally. But she stares at it like it has betrayed her.)

Big Coulee sets the babies down and turns to Carson. Her flower crown is over both eyes now.

"You raced like a champion," she says. "AND you played fair when you could have stolen the last baby. AND you let it tie. Do you know how rare that is, child?"

Carson shrugs.

Big Coulee gives him the Tusk-Charm. Then she does something Pip-Pip has never seen her do: she breaks a small piece off the corner of her own tusk and adds it to the charm with a thread of grass. The charm is heavier now. It glows faintly.

"That," she says, "is so you can call me. I'll come. I'll come ANYWHERE. I have very loud lungs and I am not afraid of the man with the clipboards."

She bumps her forehead against his, gently. The flower crown drops over his head and stays there for a moment.

"Take it," she says. "It looks better on you anyway."

Carson laughs. He puts the flower crown back on her head.

## Passage 135 — Settle the Babies First
Carson is about to say something competitive.

Then he looks at Tuft and Tumble.

The two baby mammoths are bumping into each other. Their trunks are tangled. One of them, Tumble, he thinks, is making a small worried sound. The other is trying to nurse on Big Coulee's leg and is being gently kicked off because Big Coulee is in race mode.

Tuft and Tumble are scared. They don't want their mom to leave.

Carson doesn't know how he knows this. He just does.

He says, "Race in twenty. Let's settle them first."

Big Coulee snorts. "Child. The race is in THIRTY MINUTES. I have been TRAINING."

"They're scared," Carson says. "Look at them. They don't want you to go. We'll do it. They just need to know what's going to happen."

Big Coulee stops. She looks at her babies. She had not, in fact, noticed.

She lowers herself to the ground (slowly, mammoths take a minute to lower themselves) so her huge belly is on the grass and she is no taller than the babies. She gathers them in her trunk. Tuft and Tumble bury themselves in her side.

Carson kneels next to them.

"Hey," he says. "Hey, I'm Carson. Your mom is the best racer in The Old Roar. She has been getting ready to win this race for SIX MOONS. Did you know that?"

The babies do not know. They blink at him.

"I bet you're worried she'll go too far," Carson says. "But she's not. The race is just to that pole and back. You'll be able to see her the whole time. I'll point so you know exactly where she is. Is that okay?"

Tuft puts her trunk on Carson's wrist. Tumble climbs into his lap. (Tumble is not small. She is the size of a labrador. Carson catches her.)

Big Coulee watches.

Big Coulee says, in a voice Pip-Pip has never heard her use, "Nobody ever stopped to talk to them before."

Carson shrugs. He has a six-year-old cousin. He knows what scared looks like.

They spend ten minutes calming the babies down. By the time they're done, Tuft and Tumble are leaning against Carson, half-asleep, breathing slow. Big Coulee is also calmer than she has been all morning.

The race goes ahead. Big Coulee runs. Carson runs. Big Coulee wins by a lot, Carson lost his prep time and his legs feel like wet noodles by the halfway mark. He finishes thirty seconds behind. He laughs the whole way.

Big Coulee thunders to him, scoops him up in her trunk, and brings him to where Tuft and Tumble are watching from the side.

The babies cheer.

Big Coulee sets Carson down. Her eyes are wet.

"Child," she says. "You gave up your race so my babies wouldn't be scared."

Carson shrugs again. "It wasn't going to matter for the race. They were going to be scared the whole time and you would have run worse."

Big Coulee lowers herself. She presses her enormous forehead against his. Then she does it again, more deliberately. Then she lifts the Tusk-Charm off Pip-Pip's clipboard and presses it into his hand.

"This is yours. But also." She nudges Tuft and Tumble forward. "*These* are yours when you need them. Whatever comes, whoever you need to face, *we* will be there. The whole family. You looked out for mine. I will look out for yours. That is how it works."

She lowers her enormous head once more, eye level with him. "Now I know your name, child. *Carson the Trustworthy.* Not because somebody decided. Because you earned it in front of me, with my own babies. People will follow you because they already wanted to. Remember that."

Carson can't quite speak.

Tuft puts her trunk on his shoulder. Tumble headbutts his hip gently.

Big Coulee walks him back to his boat. Tuft and Tumble walk beside him, one on each side.

## Passage 136 — Exit: Big Coulee Walks Him to His Raft

The boat-on-wheels is waiting where he left it. The grass is bent where it sat.


Before he climbs back onto the boat-on-wheels, Big Coulee stops him.

She lowers her head one more time. Her voice is small. Just for him.

"Child. The thing you did today, running hard, AND stopping for the babies, AND letting it tie, AND not pretending I was easier than I was, that's not just being a good competitor. That's a thing you do all the time, isn't it. At home. With the small one. The fishing kid."

He heads back toward the meadow, toward whoever is waiting, toward the sound of the C-130 somewhere up in the world above.

## Passage 140 — Arrival in the Rocky Wood

![](images_small/reid_region.png)

Reid's raft slides up onto a beach made of small flat stones. The raft has become a long log canoe, hollowed out, the inside smooth from many hands. He steps off without looking, his feet already know.

The wood in front of him is ancient.

The trees are the size of houses. The trunks are wider than his arms could reach around three times over. The bark is so thick and so old it has its own bark. Light comes through the canopy in long slow shafts. The air smells like turned earth and old cedar and a specific cold sweetness Reid has never met before and somehow already knows.

He bends. He picks up a fallen branch. He starts whittling without deciding to.

A voice from a stump says, "*FINALLY.*"

Reid looks up. Calmly. He has been startled by many things in his life and reacts to each one the same way.

The stump is not a stump. It is a person. Bark for skin, leaves growing where hair should be. He is enormous, twice Reid's size, but he's sitting down so he doesn't seem scary. Just *large.*

Just him. The wood is enormous around him. He is not afraid. He has been alone in big places before. He has been alone at the back dock in the dark, after everyone else has gone in, listening for the bats. He knows how to be quiet inside something quiet. Maybe more than any of the other kids, this is where he was meant to land.

"FINALLY a visitor," the tree-person says. "Wait, am I sure? I might be dreaming. Pinch me. No, don't. I'm bark, you'll hurt your fingers."

He laughs. The whole wood shakes a little. Birds Reid didn't notice fly out of the upper branches.

"I'm OLD SPEAK," he says. "Or that's what people called me a thousand years ago. I might have a new name now. I haven't checked. Sit, sit. *Visit.*"

Reid sits on a root that turns out to be perfectly shaped for sitting. He keeps whittling.

Old Speak watches him for a long moment.

"You make sticks," he says. "I like sticks."

## Passage 141 — The Just-sit Moment

![](images_small/trees_bowing.png)

Old Speak watches Reid whittle for a while longer.

"You're doing the thing," he says.

Reid looks up. "What thing?"

"The *working* thing. You always do the working thing. I can tell. I was a tree before I was a person, I've watched a thousand visitors and the working ones have a posture. Shoulders forward. Hands going. Eyes already on the next problem."

Reid is quiet. He doesn't really know what to say. He IS doing the working thing. He doesn't know how to do other things.

Old Speak says, "Put the stick down. Just for a minute."

Reid puts the stick down.

"Now look up," Old Speak says.

Reid looks up. The canopy is a hundred feet above him. Light is coming through in slow shafts. A bird he can't identify is moving from branch to branch without making a sound.

"That bird," Old Speak says, "is called Listen. She's the only one of her kind. She doesn't sing. She only listens. She's been listening to this wood for two hundred years."

Reid watches the bird. The bird watches him back.

Old Speak says, "I am ALSO listening. I have been listening to you since you stepped off your boat. You came in quiet. You don't take up extra room. You start working before you're asked because you don't want anyone to have to ask. That is a rare way to be, child."

Reid feels his face get warm.

Old Speak says, "I see you. Most don't. I just wanted to say so."

Old Speak's voice goes quieter still. "You are Reid the Noticer. The wood will remember that you saw. So will I."

Reid swallows.

Then he picks up the stick again, but slower this time.

## Passage 143 — Take the Maple, Carve Fast
Reid takes the maple. He knows maple. He's made twenty walking sticks out of maple over twenty summers. His hands know the grain and the way it splits and the way it doesn't.

He carves. Old Speak watches.

The staff takes about twenty-five minutes. It is six feet long, a little thicker at the base, smooth where his hand will hold it. He sands it with a rough stone Old Speak hands him without being asked. The staff is clean and straight and a good honest piece of work.

He stands it up. He leans on it. It holds. The wood is warm under his palm.

Old Speak nods.

"That," he says, "is a fine staff. It will not break easily. It will hold the line you want it to hold. Maple is honest. So are you."

He pats the staff. Then he reaches into his bark and pulls out a tiny carving, a miniature staff, the size of Reid's finger.

"That's a memory token," Old Speak says. "Carved by me, a long time ago. If you ever come back, hold it up so I know it's you. I've forgotten faces a few times. Last visitor I had, I called him by his great-great-grandfather's name. He took it well."

Reid pockets the tiny staff.

*If the reader has played Carson's branch, there's a faint distant rumble from a long way off, a mammoth running on packed grass. Old Speak's leaves rustle once. "She's a good one," he says. "Loud, though." He does not explain.*

Reid stands. He thanks Old Speak. He walks back toward his raft.

Old Speak waves with leafy fingers.

"Tell me a new story next time you come," he calls. "I've memorized this one already. Even though we didn't tell any."

Reid laughs, just a little.

He paddles away with a maple staff that will hold a bulldozer.

## Passage 144 — Ask for the Technique
Reid says, "Can you teach me something I don't know yet?"

Old Speak's whole face does something. His leaves brighten. The bark at the corners of his mouth crinkles upward.

"*YES,*" he says.

He shows Reid the technique. It is not what Reid expected.

The technique is not about how you cut. It is about *what you think* while you cut.

"The wood is listening," Old Speak says. "It has been listening since it was a seed. It remembers everything that ever walked past it. If you carve and you think about nothing, the wood will be nothing. If you carve and you think about WHERE you are and WHO you love and WHAT this staff is for, the wood will hold all of it. Forever. That is the technique. I cannot teach you more than that. The rest is yours."

Reid takes the driftwood. (He picked it without thinking. His hand went to it.)

He carves slowly. He thinks about the dock at home. He thinks about Ella jumping off the end last summer with her eyes closed and her arms straight up. He thinks about Aiden's first cast with the back-dock fishing rod, the cork bobber landing right where Reid told him to aim. He thinks about the boards under his feet that have held a thousand bare-foot summers and would like, please, to hold a thousand more.

The driftwood opens for him. He doesn't carve it so much as *reveal* it. The shape inside is already there. He just takes away the parts that aren't.

The staff is done in an hour. It is heavier than the maple would have been. It is the color of wet basalt and the grain shows three concentric rings near the top where his hand will rest, three rings, like the third plank of the dock that wobbles when you step on it.

It hums when he holds it. Low. Like a struck bell, far away.

Old Speak is very quiet for a long minute.

Then he says, "I have not seen wood do that in seven hundred years."

He stands up. (Standing takes him a while. He is enormous.) He bows. Then he says, "I am coming with you."

Reid blinks. "To the lake-up-above?"

"To the dock that wobbles. I have not left this wood in nine hundred years. I will leave it for this. I am older than the man with the clipboards. I have stood in storms he cannot imagine. He will look me in the face and remember that he is small."

Reid says, "Are you sure?"

"Child," Old Speak says, "I am made of *wood.* I will be very dramatic. Try and stop me."

*Far off, the bark of a wooly mammoth narrating her own training carries through the canopy. Old Speak's leaves rustle once. "Loud one," he says, affectionately. "Best of the racers. Don't tell her I said so."*

Reid stands. Old Speak picks up the maple piece Reid didn't use and tucks it under his arm, "for snacking later", and follows Reid down to the beach. The canopy parts for him as he walks.

## Passage 145 — Ask About the Driftwood
Reid says, "Tell me about the driftwood. Where did it come from?"

Old Speak goes still. Then he laughs, a quiet laugh this time, not the shake-the-trees one.

"Nobody has asked me that in a thousand years. They always ask if it's the *strongest* one. Nobody asks where it came from."

He picks up the driftwood. He turns it slowly.

"This came from the FIRST TREE in The Old Roar. The first one to grow when the floods stopped roaring and the basalt cooled and there was finally enough soil for a root. It grew at the edge of what your people now call a cove. It stood there for nine hundred years. It fell in a storm. The lake took it. The lake held it for another five hundred years, water-soaking it, hardening it, weaving the grain with memory. Then the lake gave it back to me. To hold. Until the right hands came for it."

He sets the wood down in front of Reid.

"You are the right hands."

Reid swallows. He doesn't quite believe it. But the wood is warm under his palm.

He carves it slowly. He thinks the things Old Speak taught him, *where you are, who you love, what this staff is for.* He thinks about the dock. He thinks about Ella jumping off it with her arms up. He thinks about Aiden's first cast. He thinks about the *third piling*, the one he noticed earlier this morning, the one the water has been eating out from under.

He carves THAT into the staff. Not as a picture. As a knowing.

The staff finishes itself, almost. His hands move and the shape appears. At the top, three rings. At the base, a faint mark like the curve of a shoreline. The whole thing hums when he picks it up, a low, slow hum, like a chord pulled out of a deep place.

Old Speak is openly weeping. He weeps sap. It is bright amber and very sticky.

"You did the thing I cannot teach. You let the wood remember a *specific* place. That staff is now bonded to your dock. When you plant it at the base of the third piling, the wood will *root.* It will reach into the lake-bed. It will hold the soil in place. The water will move around it instead of under. Your dock will stand."

Then the wood does something Reid will remember the rest of his life.

Every tree in the rocky woodland, every single one, every trunk, every branch, every leafy hand, bends very slightly toward him. The canopy itself reorganizes. The whole forest is *bowing.* Not to the staff. To the boy who carved it.

The sunlight through the bent canopy makes new patterns on the beach where his canoe is waiting, and the patterns look exactly like the grain of the wood in his hand, scaled up a thousand times.

Old Speak says, very quietly: "Well. They've never done that for me. They like you, child."

Reid doesn't trust himself to speak.

After a long moment, he says, "Can you come with me?"

"I would not miss it," Old Speak says. "I am old. I have wanted to see a wobbly dock survive for a very long time."

He stands. He bows to Reid the way ancient things bow, slowly, with their whole body.

*If the reader has played Aiden's branch, a small bright shape passes through a high gap in the canopy, a unicorn galloping, with a small boy on her back. Old Speak watches it go. "That child found his friend," he says, satisfied. "Good. Good. Good."*

Reid walks down to the beach. Old Speak follows. The wood opens for him.

## Passage 120 — Arrival at the Driftwood Stage

![](images_small/driftwood_players.png)

Chayten's raft slides onto a grassy hill above a half-moon of water. The raft used to be a paddle board. Now it's a small driftwood boat with her initials burned into the bow. Her sandals are still wet from the dock at home.

Below her is the strangest amphitheater she has ever seen.

A natural bowl of grass curves down to the edge of the water. A long flat stage of pale driftwood juts out over the water like a tongue. The seats are stones, not stones placed there, *stones that grew there*, arranged in tidy rows by something that knew exactly how an audience should sit. Some of the stones are big. Some are huge. A few of them, the ones in the back, are larger than her house.

They are humming.

It's a low sound, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum before your ears catch it. The stones are warming up for something. They are an audience that arrived early.

She's alone. The others are elsewhere. Aiden is not here. Her stomach pulls toward wherever he is. She is very aware of how loud the Stones' humming is when there's no one beside her to share the sound with. She has not been this alone in a long time. The humming gets bigger when nobody else is there to soak it up.

On the stage, a dozen otters are running in a panic.

One of them, old, gray around the muzzle, wearing a velvet cape and a single small bandage wrapped around his whiskers, is sitting in the middle of the stage looking absolutely calm in the way of a man whose world is ending.

He sees Chayten.

He stands up very slowly.

He says, in a voice like an entire theater season ending: "*You.* You with the EYES. Get DOWN here."

## Passage 121 — The Be-a-kid Moment

![](images_small/chayten_region.png)

Chayten walks down the grass slope. The wet ones in her sandals squelch.

The old otter meets her at the foot of the stage. He bows. Then he winces, because bowing tugs his bandaged whiskers.

"OTTO," he announces. "Lead actor. Or *was*. My whiskers betrayed me at the dress rehearsal. The Stones go on in forty minutes." He gestures up at the enormous humming audience. "They have been waiting for this play for *eighty years*. They will be FURIOUS if it does not happen. You have the EYES. You will play the lead. Yes?"

Chayten opens her mouth.

She is about to say yes, because that's what she does. Saying yes when other people need something is approximately ninety percent of her personality.

But Otto holds up one paw.

"Stop. Wait. Look at me."

Chayten looks at him.

Otto squints. He tilts his head. He sees, very clearly, the way Chayten has been holding herself, like a chaperone at someone else's party, like a sister who has misplaced her brother for the first time in seven years.

"He is FINE," Otto says.

Chayten says, "Who?"

"The small one. The one you're missing. *He is fine.* He is somewhere doing something brave. You can feel him. Yes?"

Chayten checks, behind her eyes, the place where she can always feel Aiden.

He is, in fact, fine.

He is also, somehow, riding something that is not a horse.

She lets out a breath she didn't know she was holding.

"Good," Otto says. "Now. You are not a sister right now. You are not an oldest. You are not a babysitter. For one hour, you are an *actor*. Do you accept the demotion?"

Chayten laughs. It surprises her.

"Yes," she says.

Otto looks at her for one more long second. Then he says, more quietly: "And child. Before I go. May I just sit here a moment? With you. Not to ask anything."

Chayten blinks. Nobody has ever asked her that. Nobody has ever just *asked to be near her* without needing her to do something for them.

She nods.

Otto sits down on the stage beside her. Cross-legged otter style. He doesn't speak. He doesn't perform. He doesn't introduce his troupe or explain the play or check his bandage. He just sits with her, the way a person sits with another person whose company is enough.

It lasts maybe ten seconds. It is the most important ten seconds Chayten has experienced in a year. Nobody at home knows how to do this with her. They love her so much they always reach for her to *do* something.

Otto stands slowly. "And one more thing, child. You are Chayten the Connector. The one who sits with people because they need someone to sit with them. That is rarer than the world tells you. Remember the name."

"*Excellent.* Now. Follow me."

## Passage 123 — Take the Script
Chayten takes the script. The paper is soft from a thousand readings. The handwriting is small and looped.

She reads fast. The play is short, six scenes, maybe twenty minutes of stage time. It is about a young otter girl whose little brother was lost in a storm. She spends the rest of her life singing him home, every night, by the water, until she is old. Then she finds him. That's the ending.

It is a sad play.

It is also, Chayten realizes, a beautiful play.

She gets her lines down in twenty minutes. Otto fits her with the velvet cape. Another otter, a fussy one with a measuring tape, adjusts the hem in three seconds flat and tells her she has "*workable*" shoulders.

The Stones are humming louder now. The amphitheater is full. From the back, the audience looks like a quarry someone tipped on its side.

Chayten goes on stage.

She delivers the lines. She is good. She has always been good at being the center of something. Her voice carries. The Stones hum approval at the climactic line, the sister's promise to her brother, every night, that she will keep singing, and when Chayten finishes the final scene, the stones rumble like distant thunder. Otters in the wings weep. Otto, from off-stage, blots his eye with a corner of his cape.

The lights (which are not lights, they are floating bioluminescent moss balls) come up.

Otto walks out on stage and presents Chayten with a small mask carved from driftwood. The mask is shaped like an otter's face but with eyes deliberately too kind. Otto explains that this is the Otter Mask. Whoever wears it speaks with a voice the listener cannot ignore.

"You did well," Otto says. "You delivered our play."

A small otter Chayten hasn't met before, tiny, holding a paper bag, comes up and hands her something. It is a small silver fish.

"For your brother," the small otter says. "He likes fishing. The fish is for him."

Chayten kneels down. "How did you know?"

The small otter shrugs. "You said his name when you got nervous."

Chayten tucks the fish in her pocket.

*If the reader has played Madison's branch, the small otter pauses and tilts her head toward something far off. "Did you hear that?" she whispers. "Someone is dancing on water." Chayten listens. She thinks she hears, very faintly, footsteps on stone, the precise sound a ballerina makes when she lands.*

She leaves with the mask in her hands, the fish in her pocket, and the feeling that she did something well.

It was a good show.

It was not a great show.

She is not sure why she feels that.

She thinks she might know.

She doesn't quite say it out loud.

## Passage 124 — Ask What the Play Is About
Chayten doesn't take the script yet.

She looks Otto in the eye.

"What's it really about?"

Otto goes very still.

The other otters, who had been swirling around them with capes and combs and measuring tapes, stop. They all turn slightly to look at Otto. As if they are waiting to see what he says.

Otto sits down on the stage. He pats the wood next to him. Chayten sits.

He takes a long breath.

"It is about my brother," he says. "Not the brother I had. The brother I *chose*. We were not born to the same mother. We were not the same kind of otter. He was river. I was lake. We were raised together because his mother died and mine had room. We were brothers anyway. We were not blood. We were better than blood. Do you understand?"

Chayten thinks of Aiden, who is her brother by blood.

Then she thinks of the seven other kids at Sun Lakes. Who are her brothers and sisters in a different way. By choice. By return. By every July for as long as she can remember.

"Yes," she says.

Otto nods. "He was lost in a storm when we were small. The play says the sister sings him home. The truth is, I never sang him home. I just kept singing because I didn't know what else to do."

He looks at her with old wet eyes.

"I have played this play seven hundred and twelve times," Otto says. "Every time, I am the brother who comes back at the end. Every time, I think, *what if this is the time he hears me.*"

He laughs at himself. It is a small, soft laugh, the kind that doesn't ask to be joined.

"The Stones know," he says. "That's why they hum so loud. The play is real."

Chayten thinks for a moment.

Then she says, "Can I change the ending?"

Otto blinks. "Change it?"

"Just one line. At the very end. When the sister finds the brother. Instead of saying *you came home*, can I say *you were always with me*?"

Otto is quiet for a long time.

He says, "I have been playing the wrong ending for seven hundred years."

Then he hugs her. It is the kind of hug only an old otter can give, small, surprisingly strong, and slightly damp around the edges. His whiskers tickle her ear. He smells like cedar and old velvet.

They rehearse together. He plays the brother. She plays the sister. The new ending makes both of them cry the second time through. By the fourth time they are laughing because they keep crying.

The performance is beautiful. The Stones hum until the ground shakes. The audience of otters in the front weeps openly. Two of them have to be carried out. One says, "WORTH it."

After the play, Otto presents Chayten with the Otter Mask, except this version is older, more worn, more loved. "Mine," he says. "Now yours. Speak with it and the listener cannot pretend not to hear."

He also says, very carefully: "I will come with you to the lake-up-above. To the dock. I have not left The Old Roar in two hundred years. But I'll come for this. For you. For what you did."

A small otter (Pickle, Chayten learns) gives her a silver fish for Aiden. Pickle is shy and won't quite meet her eye.

Chayten leaves with the mask, the fish, an old otter shouldering a velvet cape behind her, and a feeling she will not put into words for several days.

## Passage 125 — Notice Pickle First
Chayten was about to take the script.

But there's a small otter shaking by the wardrobe trunk.

She is tiny. Half the size of the other otters. She is holding a costume in both paws, a child-sized brown smock, and she is staring at it like it might bite her.

Chayten kneels down.

"Hey."

The small otter looks up. Her eyes are enormous.

"Hey," Chayten says again. "I'm Chayten. What's your name?"

"...Pickle."

"Pickle. Are you in the play?"

Pickle shakes her head fast. Then she nods. Then she shakes her head again. "I'm, they said I could try out. I tried out. They said I could be the *brother*. The little brother. Just the early scenes. But I can't, I can't do it, I can't go out there, my legs are, they're not, they don't work, they're wrong, I can't , "

Chayten puts a hand on Pickle's shoulder.

"Hey. Pickle. Look at me."

Pickle looks.

Chayten says, "You don't have to be brave to be useful. You just have to show up."

Pickle blinks twice.

"Will you go out there with me?" Chayten asks. "I'm playing the sister. You'd be playing my brother. We'd be on stage together the whole time. I won't let go of your paw. Not for one second. Deal?"

Pickle says, in a voice so small Chayten has to lean in: "...Deal."

Chayten stands up and walks back to Otto.

She says, "I'll do the play. But I want Pickle as the brother. Both scenes. The young one AND the old one."

Otto frowns. "Pickle is too small for the older scenes. The brother grows up. We have a, we have a *taller* otter for that role , "

"Pickle is the brother," Chayten says. "Both halves. Or I won't do it."

The other otters go very quiet.

Otto looks at Chayten. He looks at Pickle, who has straightened her tiny spine half an inch.

Otto says, "I have been mis-casting that role for forty-three years."

He turns and roars at the troupe: "PICKLE IS THE BROTHER. RESET THE BLOCKING. *MOVE.*"

The troupe moves.

Chayten and Pickle take the stage holding paws. Chayten does not let go for the entire play. Not once. Pickle delivers her lines in a tiny voice. The big Stones in the back lean forward to hear, Chayten can FEEL them lean, and when Pickle says her one big line ("I am still here, I am still here, I am still here, I never went away"), the Stones in the back hum so deep that Chayten's chest vibrates with it.

The play ends. Pickle is crying. Chayten is crying. Otto, from the wings, has fallen over.

The Stones hum for a long minute after the curtain.

Otto comes out and presents Chayten with the Otter Mask, the oldest one, the one his great-grandmother wore. "Whoever wears it is heard," he says. "But you didn't need it tonight. You were already heard. That is rare."

Pickle climbs up Chayten's side and clings to her shoulder like a small wet stole. "Can I come with you?" she whispers. "I want to come with you."

Chayten says, "Yes."

Then one of the back-row Stones, the biggest one, the one that hasn't moved in five hundred years, says, in a voice like a quarry waking up: "**SHE SAW THE SMALL ONE FIRST. THAT IS A REAL THING.**"

The whole troupe goes still.

Otto whispers, "The Stones haven't *spoken* in a hundred and twenty years."

Chayten doesn't know what to say.

So she just bows.

The Stones hum.

Pickle gives her a small silver fish for Aiden. (Pickle had been saving it. She had been waiting for someone like Chayten.)

Chayten leaves with the mask, the fish, Pickle on her shoulder, and the weird permanent feeling that the biggest Stone in the back was speaking specifically to her.

## Passage 150 — Arrival at Skip-stone Lake

![](images_small/kellen_region.png)

Kellen's raft slides up to the edge of a long flat lake. His raft is now a sleek racing canoe with carbon-black sides. He steps onto the shore and a stone skims past his ear.

Bounces nine times across the water.

He turns.

A flock of bird-creatures stand on the bank, long-legged, blue-feathered, smug. They have piles of stones in front of them and a tiny scoreboard etched into a piece of slate. They are taking turns throwing.

The biggest one, tall as Kellen's knee, with white markings around her eyes, notices him.

"Kid," she says. "This is a CHAMPIONSHIP game. You can watch from over there." She points with one wing at a flat rock thirty feet away.

Just him. He likes it. He always plays better when there's nobody watching him pretend not to think. The lake is enormous. The wind is good. The birds don't know him yet. He can be all of himself before anyone has decided who he is.

Kellen says, "I'd like to play."

All the birds laugh.

The biggest bird says, "This game requires SKILL. And STRATEGY. Most kids only have one or the other. Possibly neither."

Kellen says, "I'll take that bet."

The bird looks intrigued. "It's not a bet. It's a CHALLENGE."

Kellen picks up a stone. He doesn't say anything else. He weighs it in his palm. He turns it over. He checks the flat side against his thumb.

"Hmm," she says.

Kellen doesn't look up.

Kellen nods.

He doesn't say *I'll take that bet* again. He doesn't need to.

## Passage 151 — The Whole-self Moment

Skim hops over (it is more of a hop, with her wings folded behind her like a tiny field general). She holds two stones in her beak.

She sets them in front of Kellen.

"Pick the throwing one."

Kellen looks at them. They look identical to anyone else. They are not identical to him. The one on the left is slightly flatter on the bottom, better skip surface. The one on the right is heavier, better distance but worse curve.

He picks the one on the left.

Skim nods, once. She picks up the heavier stone and skips it across the lake. Nine bounces, then a curve, then a stop. She does this without looking.

Then she does something weird. She picks up the *flatter* stone, the one Kellen would have used, and deliberately throws it badly. The stone skips twice, wobbles, sinks.

She turns to him.

"That's how it goes if I only use my throwing arm. If I forget the rest of me. Did you see?"

Kellen did see.

"I've been doing this for forty years," she says. "You know what I see most? Kids who hide one of their halves. Athletes pretending they're not smart. Smart kids pretending they're not athletic. They think they have to pick. They think people will tease them if they're both."

## Passage 155 — Invent a New Pattern

She holds it out in her cupped hands and looks at it. She is just a frightened girl with stringy hair and a too-big robe. She does not look like a queen. She looks like Ella's friend Maya from the third row of her class, who is usually a little overwhelmed but always tries.

Marigold puts the crown back on. Now she looks like a queen again.

Then she takes Ella's hand and puts it on the crown on her head.

"That's all it is," Marigold says. "Metal and shine. It's not what makes the queen. Watch."

She takes the crown off again. She sets it on a frog's head. The frog looks ridiculous.

The frog does not act ridiculous. The frog stands up straighter. The frog clears his throat. The other frogs stop arguing for a second and look at him. He says, in a small uncertain voice, "...we should, order? Maybe?"

## Passage 174 — Step Between the Fox and the Fern
Ella stands up.

She walks down the throne steps. She steps between the fox and the fern.

She is ten years old. She is four feet tall. The fox is twice her size.

The fox tilts his head and smiles a smooth fox smile. "Move along, little princess. The grown-ups are talking."

Ella does not move.

She says, in a voice that is small but is also *exact:* "You will NOT take her home."

Three things happen at once.

The water in the moss circle goes flat. Not still, *flat,* like a lid was pressed onto it from above.

The frogs in the court, every last one, even the loudest, even the protocol-yelling ones, stop arguing. They all turn at the same time and face Ella. They are no longer her audience. They are now her *court.*

And the egg-mother under the fern, who has been afraid all morning, takes the smallest deep breath she has taken in days. It is the breath of a creature who has just heard somebody, *finally,* say the thing she could not say for herself.

The fox blinks. He starts to laugh. The laugh stops in his throat because Ella is *not blinking back* and because everything in the room has just decided to be on her side.

Behind her, Ella hears the smallest sound from the egg-mother, a tiny relieved breath.

The fox tries again. "Princess, the scroll is LEGAL. It is signed. It is BINDING."

Ella says, "Show me where it's signed."

The fox shows her. He shows her the bottom of the scroll. The signature is florid and large.

Ella looks at it. She has a six-year-old cousin who forges his name on permission slips and she knows exactly what forged ink looks like, the way the pen lingers at the start of the curve, the way the loops are too perfect. This scroll has been forged.

She says, loudly enough for the court to hear: "This is a fake. The signature was added later. Look at the way the ink sits ON TOP of the seal instead of UNDER it. He drew it after the seal was already pressed."

The frogs lean in. They all look. They all see it now.

The fox starts to back up.

Ella says, still not blinking, "You will leave. You will not come back. You will not bother the egg-mother again. If you do, the Bright Water Court will know, and I will know, and we will FIND you."

The fox bows. He drops the scroll. He leaves so fast his tail bumps a frog in the head.

The frogs cheer.

The egg-mother comes out from under the fern. She is much smaller than the fox made her seem, a creature about the size of Ella's two palms together, with kind soft eyes and a chorus of tiny round eggs clinging to her back. She bows so low her forehead touches the moss.

"Princess. Thank you."

Ella kneels (which is hard in golden boat-trim shoes but she manages it) and says, "You can come home. Nobody else is taking it. I promise."

The egg-mother says, very softly, "If you ever need us. Anywhere. The whole egg-chorus and I will sing for you. We sing one note all at once. It is, it is quite something. People stop and listen."

Marigold has been silent on the throne, watching all of this. Now she stands.

"Princess Ella. You have just protected the Bright Water. You are no longer a guest in this court. You are a Queen of the Bright Water." She steps down. She places a second crown on Ella's head. "And the egg-mother and her chorus go with you. As does my royal herald. The full strength of the court is yours."

The frog herald (a small dignified frog in a sash) bows.

*A long ribbon of color drifts across the sky above the court, blue, orange, green, that hadn't been there before. The egg-mother looks up. "The painter girl. She's been at the cliff. The basalt is waking up. We have not seen the basalt wake up in a hundred years."*

Ella walks back toward her boat. The herald hops along beside her. The egg-mother (and twelve tiny rolling eggs) follow.

## Passage 175 — Co-rule
Before the fox can finish his bow, Ella turns to Marigold.

"You don't have to do this alone."

The fox tries to interrupt.

Both queens turn to him at once.

The fox stops talking.

Marigold and Ella handle the case together. They sit on the throne side by side, there's barely room, and they tag-team the fox. Marigold knows the law. Ella knows the lying. The fox crumples under both at once. He bows out faster than he came in.

The egg-mother is safe. The court is calm. The frogs are still arguing but they're now arguing about *whether* to applaud, which is at least an upgrade.

Marigold turns to Ella. She is smiling like she hasn't smiled in a week.

"That was the best court session in the history of the Bright Water."

## Passage 184 — the Cliffside
Pegasus flies them to the cliff. The cliff is steep. The rock is dark. The hole is at the bottom. They land on a small ledge. They walk to the door.

Inside, Ripple is crying. But another sound is there too. A small one. Under the crying.

Pegasus folds her wings small. Aiden walks beside her. Her horn glows just enough to see.

The cave opens up. Four doors at the back. Ripple sits in the middle. In front of one door sits a very small, very grumpy thing.

He is the size of a kitten. He has spikes that are not pointy. He looks more sad than scary.

"Do not go through my door. It is MY door. I LIVE here."

Aiden kneels. "Hi. What is your name?"

"Hush."

"Hush. I am Aiden. We need to go through your door. Ripple's mom is waiting."

Hush starts to cry. His tears are tiny and glowing.

"Everyone wants to go through my door. No one ever just SITS with me. I have been here for FIVE HUNDRED YEARS. No one has ever sat with me."

Aiden sits down. He looks at Pegasus. Pegasus nods.

"I will sit with you. Will you sit with me?"

Hush stops crying mid-sob. "...Yes? Yes. *Yes.*"

Aiden opens his arms. Hush climbs into his lap. He weighs almost nothing. He smells like cave moss. He puts his small grumpy face on Aiden's chest. He shakes.

They sit. Ripple watches from the middle of the cave. Quiet.

Then Pegasus says a thing Aiden did not see coming.

"Aiden. Listen. The Hush has been alone for five hundred years. *Five hundred.* He cannot come back with us. He is tied to this cave. The door only opens because *he* opens it. If he leaves, the door closes for good. The cave seals up. No lost creature will ever be found again."

Aiden looks down at Hush. Hush is so quiet on his chest.

"But." Pegasus says it soft. "He could come if *I* stay."

Aiden looks up.

"A unicorn for a doorkeeper. Magic for magic. The cave would stay open. The Hush would get to come with you. But I would have to stay."

Aiden's eyes fill up. He does not want to pick. He wanted Pegasus to come home so much. He has not let himself think about anything else for an hour.

Pegasus lowers her head. "It is your choice, friend. I will be glad with either one."

The cave is very quiet.

Aiden looks at Hush. He looks at Pegasus. He looks at Ripple in the middle.

He thinks for a long minute. Longer than seven-year-olds usually think about a thing.

Then he says, quiet: "Pegasus, you said I was exactly right. I think being exactly right means I do not get to keep you just for me today."

Pegasus closes her eyes.

Aiden says, "But, can I think one more second?"

Pegasus says, "Take all the seconds."

Aiden thinks. He looks at Hush. Then he says: "Hush. Could you come for one day? Just one? To help us at the dock-up-above? And then come back? Would that work?"

Hush sits up. He looks at Pegasus. Pegasus looks surprised. Then *happy.*

"You, you could leave for ONE DAY?" Pegasus asks Hush. "Not for good?"

Hush thinks. "One day is a lot less than five hundred years. I can do one day."

Pegasus says, "Then the door stays open for that day. Magic lets a visit. It does not let a move."

Aiden laughs. He fixed a problem that was not supposed to be fixed.

Hush climbs to Aiden's shoulder. He has gone from sad to *brave* in about three minutes. He sits taller on Aiden's shoulder than he has sat in five hundred years. He even tries a small spike-stretch. He has never done that before. He wants to look a little tougher.

"Look," he whispers to Aiden. He sounds wowed at himself. "I am, I am DOING things. I never DO things. You made me do things."

Aiden says, like it is plain fact, "You did. You were always going to. You just needed someone to ask."

Hush thinks about that for a long moment. It is the kind of moment a small creature has when something inside him moves over for good.

Pegasus bumps Aiden's head with her horn.

"You found the third way," she says. "Most grown-ups can't. Most kids can. You just did. And look. You made him brave by treating him like he was brave already."

Aiden grins. He did not think he had made anyone brave. But he had.

*Above the cliff, a small flat stone skips across the sky in a clean pattern. Hush tilts his small grumpy head. "Some things fly here that should not. I never ask why."*

They come up in the meadow. Ripple's mother cries. Hush rides Aiden's shoulder. Pegasus flies them home.

### (if you skipped this cousin's chapter, read this version)

Ella doesn't have a crown. She has a sketchbook.

She pulls a small sketchbook from her pocket. She also pulls out a pencil. She does not explain why she has these. She just does. She finishes the drawing she was working on. She is ten years old and she does not stop finishing her own drawing to handle the most dangerous man on her dock.

Ella has not won the beat with a crown. She has won it by being so completely Ella that no amount of Drystone could disturb her.

Drystone makes one last try. He is desperate now. He shouts, "I want PROOF this is REAL MAGIC. PROVE TO ME magic exists. Otherwise this is all a child's TANTRUM."

Aiden walks forward. He's been at the back the whole time, holding Pegasus's mane.

His BELIEF is what makes Pegasus visible to Drystone specifically. Adults can't normally see her. But when Aiden looks Drystone in the eye and BELIEVES, fully, without hedging, that there is a real unicorn standing on the dock, his belief is strong enough to make her real for Drystone. He whistles.

Pegasus walks up the dock. Hooves clicking on the wood. Mane rainbow.

Aiden looks Drystone dead in the eye.

Drystone sees her.

Just Drystone.

He has to choose, second by second, between admitting the magic and his career.

He chooses his career.

He whispers, "I... can't... I can't put a unicorn on a permit."

Aiden says, "We know."

## Passage 598 — Crown Ending: the Dock Heals Overnight

![](images_small/dock_night.png)

That night.

Sparklers and snap-its on the dock. Brisket gone. Lake dark. Stars coming up. The bats are out, one swoops just overhead to eat the mosquitoes and Aiden flinches and laughs at the same time.

Madison shares a cookie with Aiden. Aiden gives one to Pegasus, hidden in the sunroom (the unicorn is staying for the rest of the trip, she'll go back to The Old Roar after the kids leave, but she's invited back any time).

Reid is whittling.

Kellen is throwing something into the lake.

Kiera is sketching the sunset.

Ella is wearing her invisible crown.

Carson is hyping a mini-golf rematch for tomorrow.

Chayten is on the back dock with Aiden's fishing rod, casting in the dim light. Pickle is on her shoulder.

Across the lake, faintly, music is drifting from the campground. Tonight it's a guitar. Last night, in The Old Roar, it had been opera. Aiden hums along under his breath. He has been told to wait. He is waiting. He is also paying attention.


After the sparklers burn down, the eight kids walk to the end of the dock. They look at each other. Nobody has to say it.

They jump.

All eight, at the same time, in the same way they jumped on the first day of the trip. Splash. Eight at once. The sound is the same. The lake holds them up the same.

Pegasus, in the sunroom, can hear them. She makes a sound that is the unicorn version of *good.*

Aiden surfaces first. On his own. No hand reaching for Chayten's. No one waiting to grab the back of his suit. He spits water and laughs the way he laughed on the first day of the trip, when he jumped in by himself for the first time, when *he was sure.*

The others come up around him. Eight heads bobbing.

And then, just for a second, only Aiden sees this, and he won't tell anyone for years, there's a figure on the far shore of the cove. Standing on a rock that wasn't there a moment ago. Beard the color of cedar bark. Suspenders. Beat-up canvas hat pushed back on his head.

He waves.

Aiden waves back, one small hand up out of the water, his orange life jacket holding him bobbing.

The figure tips his hat. He turns. He walks into the trees on the far bank. The rock is gone. The trees close behind him.

Aiden doesn't say anything. He just holds the moment in his chest the way you hold a small bird you don't want to scare.

In the morning, the dock will be a little stronger. The lake will still be a lake. Drystone will be gone. The data center will be canceled. Sun Lakes will hold for another summer.

And another after that. And another. As long as the kids keep coming back.

## Passage 599 — Silver Ending: Come Back Next Summer

That night.

Sparklers and snap-its on the dock. Brisket gone. Lake dark. Stars coming up.

The kids are quieter than they were at the start of the day. Something happened. They beat Drystone. They sent him home with his tablet dead and his clipboards scattered.

But they didn't get everything. They missed some pieces. There were corners of The Old Roar they never reached.

The dock foundation is still wobbling. The third piling still tilts. Madison's parents and Reid's parents have been talking quietly all evening about what to do.

But here's the thing: they're talking *together.* Chosen family and blood family, side by side. Drystone's attempt to split them did the opposite. Whatever happens to Sun Lakes now, they will figure it out together.

Aiden, on the back dock, says quietly to Chayten: "We have to come back. There's stuff we didn't see."

Chayten says, "We will. We always do."

Pegasus stays in the sunroom, hidden, visible only to the kids. She'll be there when they come back next summer. She has promised.

After the sparklers burn down, the eight kids walk to the end of the dock. They look at each other. Nobody has to say it.

They jump.

All eight, at the same time, in the same way they jumped on the first day. Splash. Eight at once. The sound is the same. The lake holds them up the same. They are aware, this time, of what they almost lost, and of what is still uncertain.

But the lake still holds. The chosen family still chose. Drystone is gone. The summer is not over.

Aiden surfaces first. On his own. The way he had on day one, that thing they will never take away from him, no matter what happens to the cove next year. He surfaced first this time, too. The smallest kid, swimming alone, completely fine.

Madison surfaces second, this time. She wipes water out of her eyes. She looks at her cousins, every one of them, blood and chosen, none of it mattering for the sorting.

"We're coming back next year," she says.

It is not a question. Nobody says no.

And then, just for a second, only Aiden sees this, and he won't tell anyone for years, there's a figure on the far shore of the cove. Standing on a rock that wasn't there a moment ago. Beard the color of cedar bark. Beat-up canvas hat pushed back on his head.

The figure tips his hat. He waits a beat, as if he's saying *I'll see you next summer, friend. There's more here to find.* Then he turns and walks into the trees. The rock is gone.

Aiden tucks the moment away with all the other important things.

# The Two Endings

## Passage 601 — The Crown Ending

![](images_small/crown_ending.png)

*Read this if Aiden's chapter was read and the deeper choice was taken.*

You went deep. You found the FULL version of what your cousin came to The Old Roar to find. You made the bravest choice when the lake offered it. You came home with an ally, an item that hums, and at least one secret tucked somewhere only you would know how to find.

The dock is safe, not just for this summer. *Forever.* The magic that came home with you doesn't fade. It quietly lives in the boards now.

Reid the Noticer's staff has rooted in the lake-bed and the third piling is now part of the lake itself. Kiera Both-at-Once's cliff, Cliffy, is across the cove, half-watching the cabin every minute of every day, his colors still visible if you tilt your head right. Carson the Trustworthy's mammoth, Big Coulee, has a worn track up the back canyon her family uses every summer that the grown-ups will never explain. Ella the Deciding Face's Bright Water frogs live under the dock now, and the kids will sometimes see the herald at dusk with a tiny scroll he's still trying to deliver. Kellen the Whole's Skip-Stone flock comes through every July, the smallest of them perched on his shoulder for a week before flying on. Chayten the Connector's otters, Otto, Pickle, and the Driftwood Players, leave small smooth things on the rocks at dawn, one for each cousin, never explained. Madison the Dancer's Canyon Singer keeps singing across the cove on still nights, and Aiden the Exactly-Right's mother, who is shyer than she pretends, finally answers her, the first summer Aiden is in middle school, with a small voice from the back deck nobody else hears. Pegasus visits Aiden in the sunroom every full moon and goes back to the world to find her next rider.

The cousins are the keepers of a secret bigger than the cabin. They will be the keepers of it for the rest of their lives. When the youngest of them is forty, they will still gather on the third of July, jump from the dock at the same instant, surface together, look at each other, and silently confirm: *we remember. We were there. It was real.*

Every summer their own children will run to the dock and jump in at the same instant, not knowing yet what is holding the boards up.

The summer always starts the same way. And the summer always will.

## Passage 602 — The Silver Ending

*Read this if some cousins went deep and some took the quicker path, or if a cousin's chapter went unread.*

You went somewhere in the middle. Some of the cousins came back with the deeper version of what they needed. Some came back with the simpler one. A couple of allies walked back with you. The choices were generous but not as deep as they could have been.

The dock stands. Drystone has been chased off. The wobbly third plank is fixed (mostly). The cove is safe this summer.

Some of the magic stayed asleep. The deepest secrets in the cousin's region didn't open for you this time. The creature you met there will dream of *you* for years, wondering what would have happened if you had gone one choice further. You will dream of them, too.

But the cousins all remember. They will, every summer, jump from the dock at the same instant. They will, every summer, swim across to the cliff face and touch it. They will, every summer, leave a small thing on the rocks for the otters, a piece of cookie, a stone, a piece of driftwood, and they will never tell anyone why.

One day, maybe next summer, one of them will go back into The Old Roar and find the path they missed. The dock will hold until they do.
